\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Web agents can perceive a page in two standard ways that are complementary in principle: the page's text (an accessibility tree) is precise, and a screenshot shows what the text omits. A third option fuses them, overlaying numbered marks on the screenshot and listing the marked elements as text \citep{yang2023som,koh2024visualwebarena}. Practitioners typically pick one of the three for a whole deployment. We ask the question this choice raises: \emph{is the choice worth making per task, and can it be made?}

Answering it requires keeping apart three quantities that a single leaderboard number merges:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Representation value}: does the screenshot change success, holding everything else fixed?
  \item \textbf{Preference reliability}: when one representation beats another on a task, is that a stable property of the task, or a property of one run?
  \item \textbf{Routing gain}: can a policy that chooses per task, using only what is known before it runs, beat the fixed choices?
\end{enumerate}
A hindsight ceiling picks, for each task, whichever representation happened to succeed. However large, it answers none of the three questions, because it rewards run-to-run luck as much as structure. We therefore rerun identical conditions and judge each against what a rerun alone changes.

\paragraph{Findings.}
(i) No choice wins everywhere: the fused mode leads clearly on classifieds, while text leads on WebArena's reddit tasks only by margins whose intervals cross zero (\S\ref{sec:deploy}).
(ii) The screenshot has a specific value that can be isolated and predicted. On the classifieds tasks that a zero-cost intent rule flags, adding the screenshot to an unchanged text payload and prompt raises success by 25--30 points for the two strongest backbones (11 for a 4B model), against 5--10 on the other tasks. The effect reproduces on a full rerun for the 235B backbone and is larger for the stronger backbones we tested. On reddit the same rule finds nothing (\S\ref{sec:screenshot}).
(iii) Per-task preferences between representations are reproducible beyond an additive difficulty model. Wherever all modes were rerun, we reject a no-interaction null conditioned on every task's success count and on every mode's success count in each run. Yet a single run measures these preferences with reliability 0.31--0.50, and a decision study projects that labels averaged over two to three runs would pass 0.5. Over all six modes, single-run labels also flip most on exactly the tasks where the choice can change success. The additive difficulty model already predicts this concentration, so any router trained on single runs inherits it. In this sense routing is least learnable where it is most valuable (\S\ref{sec:preference}).
(iv) Two lightweight routers that fix their operating point on training data show no reliable gain over the fixed representations and their random mixtures. In hindsight, only one pooled gain survives correction across curves (three modes, $p=0.001$). It comes from a router that only decides which tasks deserve the expensive mode, holds under billed cost only, and is not captured without looking at test outcomes (\S\ref{sec:routing}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
We measure text, screenshot and fused observation across three sites and four backbones, with identical reruns and with screenshot-free ablations that isolate the screenshot. We estimate the screenshot's value with a matched contrast and give an ex-ante rule that predicts it on one site. We test whether per-task representation preferences are reproducible, with a decision study of how many runs a reliable label needs. We compare deployable policies against fixed choices \emph{and their random mixtures}, the comparison a routing claim has to win. We take this baseline from model-routing benchmarks and apply it to observation choice (\S\ref{sec:routing}), and distil these comparisons into a four-step protocol (\S\ref{sec:method}). Findings (iii) and (iv) are the \emph{bounds} of our title: limits on how reliably one run labels a per-task preference and on what routers trained on such labels achieve.
