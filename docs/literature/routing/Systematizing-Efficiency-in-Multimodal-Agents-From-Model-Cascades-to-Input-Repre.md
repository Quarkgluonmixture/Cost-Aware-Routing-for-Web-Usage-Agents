# Systematizing Efficiency in Multimodal Agents: From Model Cascades to Input-Representation Routing
The proliferation of Large Language Models (LLMs) and Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) has catalyzed a paradigm shift in artificial intelligence, transitioning the field from static, text-based conversational interfaces to autonomous, goal-oriented agents capable of navigating complex Graphical User Interfaces (GUIs) and boundless web environments. As these agents evolve from operating within constrained, synthetic academic benchmarks to executing open-ended tasks in real-world deployments, the computational overhead associated with the continuous action-perception loop has emerged as a critical, often prohibitive, bottleneck. The interaction trajectory of a web or GUI agent requires the repetitive, high-frequency serialization of dense environmental states. These states range from sprawling Document Object Model (DOM) trees containing tens of thousands of tokens to high-resolution visual arrays that demand immense computational power to encode. This continuous influx of state information inherently inflates token consumption, exacerbates inference latency, and drives up API costs exponentially over long-horizon tasks.
Consequently, optimizing the cost-accuracy frontier has become a paramount challenge in agentic system design. Resolving this friction requires moving beyond the optimization of isolated model weights and toward the architectural optimization of the inference pipeline itself. Researchers have begun mapping the operational landscape of multimodal agents as a dynamic optimization problem, where cognitive load, execution time, and financial cost are continuously balanced against task success metrics. This delicate balancing act necessitates systemic routing mechanisms. Rather than treating an agent as a monolithic, single-pass entity that indiscriminately processes all inputs with its maximum capacity, state-of-the-art architectures decompose the inference pipeline into a sequence of intelligent routing decisions. These routing mechanisms govern an array of critical operational parameters: which model to invoke from a heterogeneous pool, how long to sustain computation before committing to an action, which discrete tokens to process and which to prune, which sensory modality to prioritize, and, fundamentally, how to represent the environment itself to the underlying model.
This comprehensive research report systematically characterizes the complete taxonomy of efficiency routing in multimodal web and GUI agents. It traverses the macro-level architectures of model selection and cascades, descends through the mid-level strategies of adaptive computation and speculative decoding, and examines the micro-level mechanics of token pruning and context compression. Building upon this exhaustive foundational landscape, the analysis executes a rigorous deep dive into an axis that remains significantly under-studied: input-representation routing. By examining how systems dynamically select, toggle, or transition between different serializations of the exact same underlying environmental state—such as raw HTML, parsed accessibility trees, and Set-of-Marks (SoM) visual prompts—for a fixed model, this report exposes the mechanistic trade-offs inherent in machine perception. Finally, the analysis culminates in a definitive gap statement, identifying the limitations of current static representation heuristics and explicitly defining the absence of systemic, per-task representation routing in contemporary agentic frameworks.

## Part 1: The Complete Taxonomy of Efficiency Routing in LLM and Multimodal Agents
The pursuit of computational and financial efficiency in autonomous agent deployment has generated a multidimensional landscape of routing strategies. These mechanisms share a unifying theoretical objective: to optimize the expected cost of processing a query or environmental observation while rigorously ensuring that the anticipated accuracy or task success rate meets or exceeds a predefined reliability threshold. Prior research has explored this cost-accuracy optimization frontier across eight distinct, yet frequently complementary, axes of routing.

### 1. Model Routing and Selection Across a Pool
The most ubiquitous and macro-level form of efficiency optimization involves routing discrete requests or agentic steps across a heterogeneous pool of foundation models. In this paradigm, a lightweight, highly efficient router evaluates an incoming prompt, user instruction, or environmental observation to dynamically select an expert model that offers the most favorable cost-to-capability ratio for that specific task. This approach operates on the foundational premise that not all steps within a web navigation trajectory require the immense reasoning capacity of a frontier, trillion-parameter model. Routine, low-complexity tasks—such as scrolling a page, clicking a highly prominent and unambiguous button, or typing text into a clearly labeled form field—can be successfully handled by significantly smaller, more economical models, reserving the massive, high-latency models exclusively for complex reasoning, disambiguation, and strategic planning.
Recent advancements in this domain have aggressively moved away from simple, heuristic-based selection toward sophisticated, learned routing mechanisms that map the latent space of tasks to model capabilities. For instance, the RouteLLM framework leverages extensive human preference data and data augmentation techniques to train specialized routers capable of distinguishing between queries that necessitate a strong, expensive model and those that can be resolved adequately by a weaker, cheaper alternative. By associating incoming requests with explicit cost thresholds—which determine the acceptable cost-quality tradeoff for a given application—this framework demonstrates the capacity to reduce API inference costs by over fifty percent without a statistically significant degradation in response quality. Crucially, the routers developed within this framework exhibit strong generalization capabilities, maintaining robust routing performance even when the underlying pool of LLMs is altered post-training, highlighting the viability of preference-based routing for long-term deployment.
Addressing the inherent volatility of modern production environments, where the available pool of expert models is constantly shifting due to updates and deprecations, the Cost-Spectrum Contrastive Routing (CSCR) framework maps both incoming prompts and the available models into a shared, unified embedding space. Utilizing compact, fast-to-compute logit footprints for open-source models and perplexity fingerprints for black-box APIs, CSCR trains a contrastive encoder to favor the cheapest accurate expert within adaptive cost bands. At inference time, the routing decision is reduced to a single, high-speed $k$-Nearest Neighbors ($k$-NN) lookup via a FAISS index. This highly optimized, lightweight mechanism achieves microsecond latency and improves the accuracy-cost tradeoff by up to twenty-five percent, entirely bypassing the need for expensive model profiling or retraining when the expert pool changes.
In the rapidly evolving context of multimodal Computer Use Agents (CUAs), the Adaptive Vision-Language Model Routing (AVR) framework applies this dynamic selection concept directly to GUI automation. Operating as a transparent proxy between the CUA orchestrator and the VLM pool, AVR introduces a semantic routing layer that estimates the difficulty of a specific GUI action from multimodal embeddings. It probes a smaller VLM to measure its confidence via log-probabilities, and then routes the action to the most cost-effective model whose predicted accuracy satisfies a strict reliability threshold. For agents equipped with memory, retrieved interaction history provides additional user interface context, which further bridges the capability gap and allows the small VLM to confidently handle actions that would normally trigger an escalation to a larger model. This framework projects inference cost reductions of up to seventy-eight percent while remaining within two percentage points of a baseline that exclusively utilizes massive frontier models. Furthermore, AVR integrates safety routing by escalating actions flagged as high-risk by a Visual Confused Deputy guardrail directly to the strongest model for verification.

### 2. Cascades and Sequential Escalation
While model routing frameworks evaluate a query and select a single expert upfront, cascade architectures deploy a sequential, dynamic escalation strategy. In a standard cascade pipeline, a query or observation is initially processed by the smallest, most economical model available in the system. If the base model's internal confidence in its output falls below a strictly defined threshold, or if an independent external verifier rejects the proposed output, the query is subsequently routed to the next, more capable, and more expensive model in the sequential chain. This "cheap-to-expensive" pipeline serves as a highly effective filter, ensuring that massive computational resources and expensive API calls are only expended when the task demonstrably exceeds the capabilities of the lightweight models.
The FrugalGPT framework pioneered this approach for general language tasks by systematically evaluating the highly heterogeneous pricing structures across commercial LLM APIs. FrugalGPT routes queries through a progressive pipeline of models, halting the escalation process only when a specifically trained judge or verifier determines that the current answer is of sufficient quality. This method capitalizes on the observation that for many standard queries, cheap models perform indistinguishably from expensive ones, and it has demonstrated the ability to reduce API costs by 52% to 98% depending on the complexity of the benchmark.
In the more demanding context of logical and mathematical reasoning tasks, cascades have been effectively implemented using output consistency as the primary routing and halting mechanism. For example, a pipeline might sample multiple reasoning trajectories from a weaker, highly affordable model using a mixture of different thought representations, such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and Program-of-Thought (PoT). The pipeline leverages the "answer consistency" of the weaker LLM as a direct proxy for question difficulty: if the diverse reasoning paths converge on the same conclusion, the result is accepted; if they diverge, indicating high uncertainty and complexity, the query is escalated to a stronger, more expensive model. This specific implementation demonstrated that LLM cascades can achieve reasoning performance comparable to using solely a frontier model while requiring only forty percent of the total financial cost.
To support the infrastructure required for sequential escalation in high-throughput environments, system-level optimizations such as Cascadia have been developed. Recognizing that the request routing strategy directly impacts the system load of each individual model within the cascade, Cascadia functions as a specialized serving system that jointly optimizes resource allocation, request parallelism, and routing pathways. By dynamically adapting cascade plans based on real-time LLM workload characteristics, Cascadia mitigates the latency overhead inherently associated with sequential processing, achieving up to a 4.4-fold improvement in system throughput compared to baseline cascade implementations. Furthermore, probabilistic models utilizing parametric Markov-copulas have been introduced to rationally tune the confidence thresholds of LLM cascades via continuous optimization, yielding highly favorable error-cost trade-offs even in low-sample regimes.

### 3. Early-Exit, Halting, and Adaptive Computation
Adaptive computation mechanisms route inference along the temporal or depth axis, dynamically determining precisely *when* to stop computing. Unlike cascade architectures that route inputs between entirely different models, early-exit strategies generally operate within the confines of a single architecture or a continuous agentic loop. They halt generation, visual search, or iterative refinement processes once a sufficient level of confidence, detail, or task completion is achieved, thereby preventing the expenditure of compute cycles on over-analysis.
In the domain of high-resolution GUI agents, this adaptive depth routing frequently manifests as dynamic zooming or progressive visual grounding. The DiMo-GUI framework, for example, is a training-free architecture that implements a progressive zoom-in mechanism to localize target interface regions on densely packed screens. Crucially, DiMo-GUI incorporates a dynamic halting system that enables the agent to make a definitive decision and terminate the iterative visual search process early when its confidence score surpasses a necessary threshold. This temporal routing reduces overthinking, mitigates cross-modal hallucination, and drastically cuts unnecessary computational costs by preventing the agent from infinitely analyzing microscopic UI elements once the primary target is identified.
Similarly, the Uncertainty-Aware GUI Agent (RecAgent) utilizes adaptive computation to route execution flow based on internal confidence metrics. RecAgent explicitly distinguishes between perceptual uncertainty (caused by visual noise and redundancy) and decision uncertainty (arising from ambiguous task instructions). When the agent encounters high decision uncertainty that exceeds its operational parameters, rather than guessing and executing a potentially destructive action, it utilizes an interactive module to halt autonomous execution and route the decision flow toward a human-in-the-loop fallback. This mechanism proactively reacts to high-uncertainty cases via human refinement, effectively routing the agent out of dangerous computational loops and ensuring reliable intent execution in ambiguous scenarios.

### 4. Speculative Execution and Draft-Verify Decoding
Speculative routing accelerates agentic interaction by bifurcating the execution pathway into two distinct streams: a rapid, lightweight "draft" trajectory and a rigorous, computationally heavy "verification" trajectory. This architectural approach leverages the inherent predictability of routine tasks within familiar environments to bypass the slow, expensive reasoning and observation loops typically required for novel exploration and complex planning.
The Accio framework represents a sophisticated application of this speculative paradigm specifically engineered for web agents. Accio addresses the latency and cost of the standard "render, observe, reason, act" loop by introducing an offline profiling pipeline that captures reusable trajectory structures for frequent tasks across known websites. During live runtime, Accio intercepts incoming instructions and routes the execution onto a speculative, lightweight fast-path utilizing small language models that map the free-form task descriptions onto the encoded profile patterns. If the speculative execution encounters an unexpected UI state or fails a rigorous verification check—indicating that the environment has changed or the task is novel—the system automatically falls back, or cascades, to a standard, heavy ReAct-based agent loop.
By accelerating the expensive exploratory phase of web navigation and delegating only the irreversible, transactional actions to the conventional browser-agent execution pipeline, this speculative routing strategy drastically improves performance metrics. In its accelerate mode, Accio lowers median per-task costs by nearly a factor of two and reduces end-to-end latency by over 33% while fully preserving accuracy. Furthermore, in aggregate mode, Accio reinvests the saved compute budget to execute multiple speculative trials, lifting end-to-end task accuracy by up to 16.7 percentage points within the original cost constraints of a single-trial baseline.

### 5. Token Pruning, Visual Token Reduction, and KV-Cache Compression
As multimodal agents process continuous streams of high-resolution screenshots and extensive textual interaction histories, the Key-Value (KV) cache of the underlying Transformer models grows substantially. This growth is quadratic with respect to context length, creating severe memory bottlenecks that drastically limit the agent's ability to process long, multi-step interaction sequences efficiently. Efficiency routing at this micro-level involves determining exactly which discrete tokens or spatial features are retained within the KV cache and which are permanently discarded, pruned, or skipped during the forward pass.
For dense visual inputs, frameworks such as ReVision have demonstrated that computer-use agents suffer from massive temporal visual redundancy. Because screenshots evolve only marginally between agent actions (e.g., a cursor moves, but the background desktop remains identical), encoding the entire image at every step is highly inefficient. ReVision addresses this by routing unnecessary visual tokens out of the processing pipeline through patch-level filtering directly in the token space of the multimodal LLM. This design preserves temporally distributed evidence necessary for long-horizon decision-making while significantly reducing token usage by approximately 46%. This routing mechanism allows agents to incorporate far more historical observations without exhausting context limits. Expanding on this concept, the Skip-Vision methodology routes tokens completely around Feed-Forward Network (FFN) layers if they are deemed redundant during training, and couples this with a selective KV-cache removal mechanism that explicitly excises these skipped pairs during generative decoding, reducing inference FLOPs by up to 75%.
In the specific context of GUI interfaces, the GUI-KV architecture capitalizes on the rigid spatial structure and predictable temporal persistence of user interfaces. GUI-KV routes tokens into or out of the KV cache by continuously calibrating retention scores against subspace-aware spatial profiles and cumulative temporal stability metrics. It recognizes that the overarching layout of an application often persists across many sequential steps, whereas specific, actionable widgets may only matter transiently. By mathematically exploiting these GUI-specific redundancies, GUI-KV reduces decoding FLOPs by nearly 40% while simultaneously increasing step accuracy by 4.1% over full-cache baselines, illustrating that intelligent token routing acts not only as a compressor but also as an effective denoiser that focuses the model's attention.

### 6. Prompt and Context Compression
While token pruning operates dynamically and mathematically inside the model's self-attention architecture, prompt and context compression routing occurs upstream, actively and semantically selecting which segments of the environmental observation or instruction history are fed into the context window in the first place.
The foundational LLMLingua framework established the paradigm for text-based prompt compression by employing an explicit budget controller and a token-level iterative algorithm. It routes only the most semantically integral and high-entropy tokens into the final prompt, achieving up to 20x compression ratios while maintaining semantic integrity and task performance.
In the web agent domain, the sheer volumetric explosion of HTML DOM trees necessitates highly aggressive context routing. FocusAgent addresses this by utilizing a soft-retrieval strategy. It deploys a smaller, highly efficient retriever LLM to evaluate raw, sprawling environmental observations, prompting it to route only the most task-relevant lines of code into the operational context of the primary reasoning agent. This approach successfully prunes over 50% of the observation size. Concurrently, this context routing functions as a critical security mechanism; by strictly filtering the input context based on task relevance, FocusAgent drastically reduces the attack success rate of prompt-injection payloads hidden within pop-ups or dynamic banners (dropping attack success rates from 90.4% to 1.0%), highlighting the dual-use nature of efficiency routing.
Operating on alternative web serializations, the A11y-Compressor framework focuses on accessibility trees. It routes the raw, linearized accessibility tree through a systematic transformation pipeline encompassing modal detection, redundancy reduction, and semantic structuring. This pipeline effectively routes out duplicated UI elements, verbose content, and off-screen background components that hinder the agent's ability to identify correct interaction targets. The resulting compact representation utilizes merely 22% of the original token count while preserving the crucial structural context and spatial relationships necessary for accurate UI grounding, improving task success rates by 5.1 percentage points on benchmarks like OSWorld.

### 7. Modality Selection and Perception Routing
As agents evolve into natively multimodal entities, the decision of whether to rely on vision, text, audio, or a complex fusion of these streams introduces a profound routing axis. Uncontrolled, omni-modal processing is computationally wasteful. Furthermore, it frequently triggers cross-modal hallucinations, wherein an agent may experience "modality dominance bias"—relying overwhelmingly on textual cues while overriding or ignoring explicit factual signals presented in visual evidence, or vice versa. Perception routing mechanisms dynamically evaluate the incoming task and the current environment to actively select the optimal sensory modality.
The AMuFC framework, explicitly designed for complex fact-verification, exemplifies this by deploying an Analyzer agent that actively determines whether visual evidence is strictly necessary to evaluate a given claim. If visual evidence is deemed redundant, the framework routes the task exclusively through a highly efficient, text-only Verifier pathway, adaptively incorporating the heavy multimodal processing pathways only when the textual data is insufficient to reach a verdict. In massive-scale video retrieval systems, LLM-based routers evaluate the user query to route perception pathways among OCR text extraction, visual scene captioning, or high-level narrative fusion, successfully reducing computational overhead by 41% while maintaining robust modality selection accuracy across millions of clips.
Within the realm of GUI automation, the VistaGUI (MP-GUI) architecture advances perception routing through specialized modality extractors. It dynamically routes the processing of the screen through graphical, textual, and spatial pathways, actively selecting and combining these modalities via an adaptive fusion gate that is tailored to the specific preferences of the immediate GUI task. This active perception routing ensures that agents do not blindly process massive image tensors when a simple API query, DOM lookup, or targeted OCR extraction would suffice to identify the target element, drastically improving the robustness and efficiency of GUI automation.

### 8. Input, Observation, and Representation Routing
The final, and arguably most granular and foundational axis of optimization involves selecting precisely *how* to present the exact same underlying environmental state to a fixed model. A single web page or application screen can be validly serialized as raw HTML, a structurally parsed DOM, an Accessibility Tree (A11y), a raw pixel-based screenshot, or an augmented screenshot overlaid with visual markers. Each of these representations fundamentally alters the agent's action space and imposes vastly different cognitive loads on the reasoning engine. While historically treated as a static architectural choice made prior to deployment, emerging research demonstrates the critical necessity of dynamically routing these representations based on the immediate context, the model's capability, and the density of the task. This specific dimension constitutes the deep dive of this report.

## Part 2: Deep Dive into Input-Representation Routing for Web and GUI Agents
The fundamental interface between an autonomous digital agent and its environment is the observation representation. A modern web page or a mobile GUI is an inherently multimodal construct characterized by deep semantic hierarchies (the underlying HTML/CSS code) and highly complex spatial layouts (the rendered visual pixels on the screen). To formulate decisions and interact with this environment, an agent must ingest this state, process it through its neural architecture, and output an executable action. For a fixed model deployed in production, the choice of how to serialize this state is not merely an arbitrary data formatting decision; it profoundly dictates the agent's reasoning pathway, its token consumption, its latency, and its susceptibility to catastrophic grounding failures.

### The Anatomy of the Observation Space
Prior to the advent of dynamic routing, frameworks generally forced models to operate on one of several static representations:

1. **Full HTML / DOM:** The raw Document Object Model provides exhaustive, unparalleled structural and semantic detail. It includes invisible attributes, scripts, computed styles, and hierarchical nesting. While this provides rich layout cues and extensive action grounding capabilities for highly advanced models, it is notoriously verbose. A typical modern webpage DOM can easily exceed 15,000 to 50,000 tokens. This volume rapidly exhausts context windows, forces models to suffer from the "lost in the middle" retrieval phenomenon, and dramatically increases inference latency and hallucination rates for lower-capability models that struggle to parse the noise.
2. **Accessibility Tree (A11y):** The A11y tree is a simplified, derivative structure exposed natively by the browser intended for assistive technologies (e.g., screen readers). It strips away purely formatting tags (`<div>`, `<span>`, `<style>`) and retains only interactive elements, their specific roles, and their current states. This reduces the token footprint drastically, often compressing 15,000 tokens of HTML down to a mere 200–400 tokens. However, this extreme compression comes at a severe cost: the A11y tree frequently discards crucial two-dimensional spatial relationships and layout context. This renders A11y-based agents brittle when UI elements lack proper ARIA labels or when tasks require complex visual-spatial reasoning (e.g., "click the icon located to the immediate right of the red banner").
3. **Visual Screenshots:** Vision-only agents bypass the structural codebase entirely, relying on Multimodal LLMs to parse the rendered pixels directly. This approach perfectly aligns the agent's perception with the human user experience, entirely bypassing the complexities of malformed HTML, obfuscated iframes, and shadow DOMs. Nevertheless, raw screenshots incur massive computational overhead due to the encoding of high-resolution image patches. Furthermore, they struggle with microscopic icon disambiguation and force the model into the notoriously difficult task of outputting exact $x, y$ numerical coordinates for interaction, a mechanism that is highly sensitive to minor layout shifts and scaling issues.
4. **Set-of-Marks (SoM) / Visual Prompting:** This hybrid representation injects semantic structure directly into the visual field. By executing client-side JavaScript to overlay numeric or alphanumeric tags (bounding boxes) on all interactable elements within the screenshot, SoM allows the agent to reason visually while outputting a discrete, categorical action (e.g., "Click element #45") rather than generating arbitrary Cartesian coordinates.

### Routing Among Textual Serializations: Capability-Aware Selection
For a fixed model traversing the web, determining whether to serialize the page as full HTML or as a compacted Accessibility Tree represents a profound cost-accuracy tradeoff. Historically, frameworks hardcoded this choice during development. However, the paradigm is shifting toward dynamic, capability-aware routing of the serialization itself.
In their foundational study, *"Read More, Think More: Revisiting Observation Reduction for Web Agents"*, Enomoto, Oyamada, et al. systematically analyze the impact of providing full HTML versus compacted A11y trees. Through rigorous controlled comparisons, they demonstrate that the optimal observation representation is not universally fixed; rather, it is entirely contingent upon two intersecting variables: the inherent capability of the fixed model and the "thinking token" budget allocated for reasoning at that specific step.
The empirical findings dictate a clear routing logic. For lower-capability models, the sheer volume of raw HTML induces severe cognitive overload; these models suffer from significantly increased hallucination rates under long, noisy inputs, making the compact A11y tree the definitively superior representation. Conversely, higher-capability models (such as frontier MLLMs) actively exploit the rich layout information embedded within full HTML (e.g., inferring spatial proximity from nested `div` structures) to achieve vastly superior action grounding. Crucially, when the system dynamically allocates a larger "thinking budget"—allowing the model longer Chain-of-Thought reasoning steps to parse the observation—the performance benefits of the exhaustive HTML representation are further amplified. Based on these controlled comparisons, the authors advocate for systems that *adaptively select* the observation representation dynamically, matching the serialization to the model's capabilities and the current compute budget, rather than adhering to a static, universally applied reduction pipeline.

### Set-of-Marks and the Dynamic Routing of Perception
The introduction of Set-of-Marks (SoM) prompting fundamentally altered multimodal web agents by bridging visual perception and discrete action spaces. However, utilizing SoM inherently involves a routing choice regarding *how* to visually present the page to the agent at any given moment.
Frameworks like the *Camel Browser Toolkit* highlight the critical evolution toward "hybrid browser" agents that dynamically route their perception based on task demands. Rather than relying solely on visual coordinates or solely on parsed text, these advanced agents seamlessly switch between visual SoM representations, direct DOM manipulation, and text-based interactions. In controlled comparisons, the SoM representation proves particularly effective for tasks requiring extensive traversal of complex, nested, or visually ambiguous menus where pure coordinate prediction frequently fails. Yet, constructing the SoM itself requires a routing action: mapping DOM accessibility data (ARIA roles and bounding boxes) into a visual overlay.
Recent cybersecurity research highlights the systemic, unintended impact of these representation choices. In adversarial scenarios evaluated within the VWA-Adv benchmark, agents are exposed to malicious prompt injections hidden seamlessly within images. The architectural choice of whether the agent processes the webpage via a Set-of-Marks visual description or an underlying Accessibility Tree directly dictates the agent's susceptibility to these visual-semantic attacks, emphasizing that representation routing is simultaneously a profound security mechanism as well as an efficiency optimization.
Furthermore, research investigating text-only versus multimodal observation paradigms underscores the necessity of adaptive representations. The AdaptAgent framework demonstrates that for proprietary and open-weights multimodal web agents, utilizing multimodal demonstrations over text-only representations boosts few-shot adaptability task success rates by up to 7.21% on complex benchmarks like Mind2Web and VisualWebArena. This establishes that for adaptation and generalization, the inclusion of the visual modality is indispensable, rejecting text-only serialization defaults.

### Dynamic Toggling: Fast, Slow, and Component-Aware Perception
Perhaps the most explicit and sophisticated implementation of input-representation routing for a fixed model is found in the *iSHIFT: Lightweight Slow-Fast GUI Agent with Adaptive Perception* framework. iSHIFT addresses the fundamental dichotomy in agentic design between the need for highly efficient, rapid high-level task execution and the strict requirement for precise, fine-grained visual grounding during critical interactions.
Rather than settling on a compromised middle-ground representation that is mediocre at both, iSHIFT enables a fixed, highly compact 2.5B parameter Multimodal LLM to dynamically route its inference between two entirely distinct perceptual modes. In the "fast mode," the model ingests a heavily compressed, global representation of the screen, utilizing these broad cues for efficient, routine reasoning and broad navigation. However, when the task demands exact visual grounding (e.g., selecting a specific, unlabeled microscopic icon in a dense cluster of similar elements), the agent deploys special "perception tokens" to actively switch the representation into a "slow mode". This slow mode routes the model's attention mechanisms to a localized, highly detailed, high-resolution visual representation of specific, targeted screen regions. By dynamically routing the observation representation between macro-textual/global cues and micro-visual high-resolution grounding, iSHIFT matches state-of-the-art performance across multiple benchmarks utilizing a fraction of the computational parameters.
Similarly, the *Uncertainty-Aware GUI Agent (RecAgent)* tackles the problem of excessive input redundancy by actively manipulating the observation space before it reaches the reasoning core. Utilizing a Component Recommendation module, RecAgent intercepts the raw GUI state. Instead of forcing the LLM to parse the entire DOM or process a full unedited screenshot at every temporal step, the system dynamically routes only a filtered, algorithmically recommended subset of UI elements to the agent's perception. This proactively alters the input representation complexity based on the calculated noise level of the current screen, adapting the observation to mitigate perceptual uncertainty. Furthermore, the *DiMo-GUI* architecture enhances GUI grounding by dynamically separating textual and iconic components from the source image, actively routing these distinct representations through separate analytical pathways in a divide-and-conquer strategy to completely prevent cross-modal interference before fusing the context back together for final decision making.

## Part 3: Gap Statement
The extensive review of the literature demonstrates that while efficiency routing is a rapidly maturing field, it remains heavily stratified. Significant breakthroughs have been achieved in routing tasks between models (CSCR, RouteLLM), managing token caches (ReVision, GUI-KV), and filtering context (FocusAgent, A11y-Compressor). Furthermore, detailed controlled comparisons have successfully elucidated the profound impact that different observation serializations (HTML, A11y, SoM, fast/slow visual modes) have on the success rates of fixed models.
However, despite these foundational insights, a critical systemic void remains in the architecture of autonomous multimodal agents.

### Is there any peer-reviewed SYSTEMATIC characterization of per-task input-representation routing for web agents on a fixed model?
**No.**
While recent empirical studies such as *Enomoto et al. (2026)* establish offline, static guidelines linking model capability to optimal serializations (e.g., firmly pairing high-capacity models with HTML and weak models with accessibility trees), there exists no comprehensive, peer-reviewed framework that actively and autonomously routes the observation mode on a strictly *per-task* or *per-step* basis during live inference. The literature currently lacks a mechanistic, real-time routing policy that quantitatively evaluates the instantaneous structural density, spatial layout dependencies, and semantic complexity of a given web page to dynamically output the single most cost-efficient and groundable environmental serialization (e.g., dynamically toggling between DOM, Set-of-Marks, and Accessibility Trees) for a fixed agent. Consequently, agents remain artificially constrained by deployment defaults, forced to process sub-optimal representations simply because current architectures cannot adapt their perceptual mechanisms to the shifting cognitive demands of the digital environment.
