\chapter{Noise-Floor Derivation and Scope}
\label{app:noise}

\emph{Referenced from Section~\ref{sec:noise-floor}.} A \emph{noise floor}
here is how much a success rate moves when the identical setup is simply run
again, with nothing changed. The main text states those thresholds and applies
them; this appendix derives them, and records which comparisons each one is
and is not allowed to police. That restriction matters because the commonest
way to misuse a noise floor is to do arithmetic across two rows that were
measured under different conditions.

\section{Derivation of the null spread}

Two runs of one condition are exchangeable under the null that nothing changed
between them. Let $d$ be the number of tasks on which they disagree and $n$ the
scored-set size. Each discordant task contributes $+1/n$ or $-1/n$ to the
success-rate difference with probability $\tfrac12$ each, so with
$X\sim\mathrm{Binom}(d,\tfrac12)$ counting the flips in one direction,
\[
  D = \frac{2X-d}{n},\qquad
  \mathbb{E}[D]=0,\qquad
  \mathrm{Var}(D)=\frac{4\,\mathrm{Var}(X)}{n^{2}}=\frac{d}{n^{2}},
\]
giving Equation~\ref{eq:floor-sd}, $\mathrm{SD}(D)=\sqrt{d}/n$. The one-sided
95\% point is $1.645\,\mathrm{SD}(D)$.

Two properties of this null are worth stating explicitly. It assumes \emph{only} exchangeability, with no model of the agent, the site, or the failure distribution enters. And it does \emph{not} model environment drift, which is
one-directional and which we separately observe: a restarted partial run of one
mode shows 3 flips in one direction and 0 in the other on its 26 shared tasks.
Where drift is present the true spread exceeds $\sqrt{d}/n$, so the thresholds
in Table~\ref{tab:floor-spread} are themselves lower bounds.

\section{Two floors, two questions}

The inventory carries two distinct floor quantities and they answer different
questions. Conflating them was the error that an earlier draft of our own
analysis made.

\begin{description}
\item[Set-difference floor.]
  $\mathrm{self\_drop}(a\!\to\!b)=|\{a\}\setminus\{b\}|/n$. This matches
  Equation~\ref{eq:dropone} in form, so it is the floor for \emph{ceiling} claims, that is, what adding one more arm buys. Measured range across the six
  replicated \cell{cls$\cdot$B0} arms: 4.46--7.59\pp.
\item[Mean-difference floor.] $|\mathrm{SR}(a)-\mathrm{SR}(b)|$ on the same
  pairs. This is the floor for claims comparing \emph{mean success rates}
  between modes. Measured range: 0.89--2.68\pp, but each value is \emph{one draw} of a quantity whose standard deviation is 2.14--2.53\pp, which
  is why the interpretable threshold is 3.52--4.15\pp{} rather than 2.68\pp,
  and why the arm-specific value, not the range, is what an effect on that arm
  is compared with.
\end{description}

Reading a 2.2\pp{} effect against a 2.23\pp{} ``measured floor'' compares a draw
to a draw. The main text uses the threshold, not the band.

\section{Scope restrictions}

\paragraph{Do not do arithmetic across rows.} Each floor carries its own scope: backbone, site, mode, $n$, and serving path. The only comparisons drawn
anywhere in this dissertation are within one (model, site) cell at equal arm
count.

\paragraph{The WebArena row is not a floor for its own baseline.} It pools five
mode-specific pilot-versus-full comparisons over the same 10 registered pilot
tasks ($\text{pooled }n=50$ over ten distinct tasks), while the distinct-arm
figure beside it uses 104 tasks starting from \mDOM{}. \mDOM{}'s own
pilot-versus-full comparison shows zero flips in either direction; the 2--4\pp{}
band is generated entirely by \mVis{} and \mPP{}, and the five panels are
correlated through the same ten tasks. It should be read as \emph{some arms of
this cell move by 2--4\pp{} under repetition}.

\paragraph{The five-arm gain is never set against a one-rerun floor by
subtraction.} We hold one rerun arm, not five, and repetition has its own
diminishing returns. The ceiling is reported with its arm count attached
(Table~\ref{tab:oracle}, Figure~\ref{fig:ceilings}); the like-for-like one-arm
comparison is Figure~\ref{fig:f10b}; and the six-arm question is answered by
bound rather than by subtraction in Section~\ref{sec:six-vs-six}, derived below.

\section{The six-rerun projection}
\label{sec:app-union}

Equation~\ref{eq:union} follows from the same null. Write $U(k)$ for the
number of distinct tasks solved at least once across $k$ runs of one mode:
$U(1)$ is what a single run solves, $U(2)$ what two runs solve between them,
and so on. Suppose the mode solves $s_0$ tasks every time, and a further $m$
tasks, call these \emph{flippable}, with probability $p$ each.
Over $k$ independent runs a flippable task is solved at least once with
probability $1-(1-p)^{k}$, so
$\mathbb{E}\left[U(k)\right] = s_0 + m\left(1-(1-p)^{k}\right)$.

\emph{Two runs do not identify $p$.} A replicate pair supplies two numbers:
$|A| = s_0 + mp$ and $\mathbb{E}[d] = 2mp(1-p)$. Three unknowns, two equations.
Solving the pair for $s_0$ and $m$ at a given $p$ and substituting, with
$q = 1-p$ and $1-q^{k} = (1-q)(1+q+\dots+q^{k-1})$, the $m$-dependence cancels:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}\left[U(k)\right] \;=\; |A| \;+\; \frac{d}{2}\left(1 + q + q^{2} + \dots + q^{k-2}\right).
  \label{eq:union-identified}
\end{equation}
Feasibility bounds $p$ from both sides: $s_0 \ge 0$ forces
$p \le 1 - d/(2|A|)$, and $s_0 + m \le n$ forces $p \ge d/(2(n-|A|))$.

Three properties follow.

\emph{The fair-coin value is not an upper bound.} Equation~\ref{eq:union-identified}
is increasing in $q$, hence decreasing in $p$. Setting $p=\tfrac12$, which is
what $\hat m = 2d$, $\hat s_0 = |A|-d$ amounts to, therefore selects a point
low in the feasible range, not above it: 12--22\% of the way up each arm's
$U(6)$ range from its lower endpoint. No distribution over $p$ is assumed, so
this is a position within an interval rather than a quantile. Table~\ref{tab:six-vs-six} reports the range alongside
the point, and Section~\ref{sec:six-vs-six} draws only the conclusion that holds
across all of it.

\emph{The $k=2$ comparison cannot test $p$.} At $k=2$
Equation~\ref{eq:union-identified} reads $U(2) = |A| + d/2$, with no $q$ in it:
every $p$ predicts the same two-run union. The residual against the observed
union is algebraically $(|A|-|B|)/2$, half the difference between the two runs'
success counts. It is worth reporting: model minus observed comes to
$-1.12$, $+0.45$, $+1.34$, $+1.12$, $+0.67$ and $+0.67$\pp{} across the six
replicated arms in the order of Table~\ref{tab:six-vs-six}, so the union
arithmetic and the marginal are both stable. It is not, however, evidence for the
flip model's shape.

\emph{It rests on one cell.} \cell{cls$\cdot$B0} is the only cell with
replicated arms, so the residual and the interval are statements about that
cell. All six of its arms now carry a replicate. We do not extrapolate, and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} lists the replicates that would be needed to.
Letting every task carry its own $p_i$ rather than sharing one $p$ does not
widen the interval. Minimising and maximising $U(6)$ over arbitrary $\{p_i\}$
subject to the same two moment constraints returns the same endpoints, since
$s_0 \ge 0$ and $s_0 + m \le n$ are what bind at each end; the homogeneous
model is not a restriction here.

\section{The determinism claim we retracted}

Our project record previously held that the locally served backbones were
deterministic across reruns, on the grounds that decoding is greedy
(\texttt{do\_sample}$=$false) and step-level outputs had been verified identical
in a controlled check. That verification was correct and the inference from it
was not. An episode also depends on site state, wall-clock and session lifetime, not only on model output; the WebArena pilot-versus-full overlap shows flips on
\mVis{} and \mPP{} despite greedy decoding. Step-level determinism and
episode-level determinism are different properties, and only the first was ever
evidenced.
