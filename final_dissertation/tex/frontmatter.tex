\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
\markboth{Abstract}{Abstract}

A web agent must be given some encoding of the current page before it can act.
That encoding can be cheap structured text, an expensive annotated screenshot,
or a combination, and the choice is normally made once and applied everywhere.
Because a task takes tens of steps, the choice is paid for tens of times. This
dissertation asks whether the expensive choice is necessary at every step, and
whether the steps where it \emph{is} necessary can be identified cheaply enough
for the identification to be worth making.

We build a controlled representation space in which six observation modes vary
the page text payload, the prompt framing, and the presence of an annotated
image while the task, the agent, the action space, and the accounting boundary
are held fixed. Across 8 (site, model) cells spanning two benchmarks
(VisualWebArena and WebArena) and three backbones, we measure three quantities
in sequence: how much a retrospective oracle could gain by choosing per task,
whether that need is predictable from serving-time features under a fully
nested cross-validation protocol, and whether acting on those predictions beats
a trivial fixed policy once cost is counted.

The answers are, in order, \emph{yes}, \emph{no}, and \emph{the question is
mis-posed}. A retrospective oracle over the six modes gains
$+3.45$ to $+16.35$ percentage points of task success over the best single mode
while spending 13.7--35.3\% less, in every cell and on both benchmarks --- the same
protocol also shows that adding a single \emph{rerun} of one unchanged mode, as
one extra arm, buys 2.0--7.6 points on its own, so the ceiling must always be
quoted beside its own repetition baseline rather than net of it. Under a fully
nested protocol the learned triage router Pareto-beats the trivial
always-cheapest fixed policy in \textbf{0 of 8} cells; so, we show, does the
retrospective oracle in 7 of 8, which recasts the negative result from ``the
learner failed'' to ``no profitable operating point exists under this cost
boundary''. The mechanism is a supply constraint rather than a capacity one:
routing labels are minted only when a task is solved, base success is 2--27\%,
and 4 of 6 cells therefore contain no trainable which-mode classifier at all. We
further show that the single feature carrying most of the apparent
discriminative power on VisualWebArena is the benchmark's own human difficulty
annotation, which no deployment possesses.

The contribution is not a router. It is a measured boundary: the conditions
under which representation routing cannot be learned, established with the
opportunity first proven to exist and the failure located in a named mechanism.

% ---------- public access ----------------------------------------------------
\chapter*{Data, Code and Public Access}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Data, Code and Public Access}
\markboth{Data, Code and Public Access}{Data, Code and Public Access}

Every component of this study is publicly obtainable with one stated exception.
The benchmarks (VisualWebArena, WebArena) are open-source and self-hosted from
their published Docker images; two of the three backbones
(\texttt{Qwen3-VL-4B}, \texttt{google/gemma-3-4b-it}) are openly available model
weights run locally; the experiment harness, analysis pipeline and figure
scripts are released with the submission.

\textbf{The exception is the large backbone.} \texttt{Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B} was
served through a metered API proxy rather than self-hosted. Its outputs,
per-step logs and billing records are archived and released, but a third party
cannot re-run those conditions without equivalent proxy access. Results that
depend on that backbone alone are therefore \emph{replicable from our logs} but
not \emph{independently reproducible end to end}, and are marked as such where
they carry a claim. Chapters~\ref{ch:repr}--\ref{ch:e2e} are constructed so that
no load-bearing claim rests on a single backbone.
