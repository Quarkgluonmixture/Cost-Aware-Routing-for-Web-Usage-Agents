\chapter{Representation Value: How Much Is There to Route?}
\label{ch:repr}

\chapterhandoff{Chapter~\ref{ch:system} fixed a boundary in which representation
is the only thing that varies. This chapter asks whether that variation is worth
anything: do the modes succeed on different tasks, how much would perfect
retrospective selection be worth, and --- the question that turns out to govern
the answer --- how much of that is worth more than simply running one mode
twice?}

Three observations follow directly, and each constrains what can be claimed
later.

\paragraph{Base success is low, and this is the regime the dissertation is
about.} Single-mode success spans 0.45\% to 27.23\% on VisualWebArena and
reaches 35.58\% on WebArena. Every subsequent conclusion is scoped to that
range; Section~\ref{sec:regime-boundary} states what would change outside it.

\paragraph{The image-free modes do not replace the image-bearing one.} On four
of six VisualWebArena cells \mSoM{} is the strongest single mode, and on
\cell{cls$\cdot$B1} it beats the best image-free mode by 6.70\pp. Any reading of
the phantom modes as ``\mSoM{} without the cost'' is contradicted by this table,
and we do not advance one.

\paragraph{The \cell{red$\cdot$B2} row is anomalous and we do not smooth it.}
\mPP{} scores exactly zero and \mDOM{} outperforms \mSoM{} by 2.96\pp. At $n=203$
with 8 total successes, the row is dominated by counting noise, and its
appearance in later analyses --- including as the one cell whose learned router
passes a permutation test in Chapter~\ref{ch:pred} --- should be read with that
in mind.

\section{Cost does not track success}
\label{sec:cost-frontier}

If the expensive modes bought success in proportion to their price there would
be nothing to optimise --- one would simply pay for the best affordable mode.
Figure~\ref{fig:f7} shows they do not. Within \cell{cls$\cdot$B0}, \mVis{} is
the cheapest mode at \$0.0648/episode and reaches 25.00\%, while \mPS{} costs
11.2\% more and reaches 15.62\%. Several modes are strictly dominated: they cost
more than an alternative and succeed less.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_f7_cost_sr_frontier.pdf}
  \caption[Cost--success frontier per cell]{%
  \textbf{Cost against success, one panel per cell.} Non-dominated modes are
  connected; dominated modes are shown rather than hidden. Cost is
  \texttt{total\_billed\_cost\_usd} per episode for B0 and the electricity
  \texttt{total\_billed\_cost\_usd} per episode, and panels are never compared
  across the API/local boundary (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}). Seven of eight
  cells carry two or three non-dominated modes, which is the premise routing
  depends on. The exception is instructive and is discussed below.}
  \label{fig:f7}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{One cell has a single non-dominated mode, and it is the cell that
matters most later.} On \cell{cls$\cdot$B2}, \mVis{} ties \mSoM{} on success
(2.23\%) while costing 22.1\% less, and beats every other mode on both axes. The
frontier collapses to one point. That is what a regime looks like when
always-cheapest is not merely competitive but dominant, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:e2e} shows it is the general shape of the problem rather than a
quirk of one cell.

\paragraph{The price ordering is not stable across cells either.} \mVis{} is
cheapest in seven of eight cells but not on \cell{wa\_red$\cdot$B0}, where
\mDOM{} is cheapest and \mVis{} is the fourth most expensive of six. And the
spread between cheapest and dearest ranges from 11.6\% (\cell{cls$\cdot$B0}) to
77.8\% (\cell{wa\_red$\cdot$B1}), so ``the cheap mode'' and ``the expensive
mode'' name different amounts of money in different cells. Cost is an outcome of
the interaction, not a property of the representation --- which is why every
cost figure here is measured per episode rather than assumed from a
context-length calculation.

\section{The modes solve different tasks}
\label{sec:complementarity}

Success rates are marginals; routing needs the joint. Across
\cell{cls$\cdot$B0}, 97 of 224 tasks are solved by at least one mode, and 68 of
those by more than one --- so 29 tasks are solved by exactly one mode and lost
entirely if that mode is not in the portfolio. The equivalent counts for the
other cells appear in Table~\ref{tab:oracle}; the share of tasks with more than
one solver ranges from 1.5\% (\cell{red$\cdot$B2}) to 34.6\%
(\cell{wa\_red$\cdot$B0}).

The proportion of tasks that \emph{nothing} solves is the more consequential
number, and it is large: 48.1\% on the most capable cell and 92.9\% on the
weakest. Most of the benchmark is out of reach of every mode, and any policy's
room for manoeuvre is confined to the remainder.

\begin{scopebox}
\textbf{Headline, with its comparator and its cell set attached.} A
retrospective oracle over six observation modes improves task success by
$+3.45$ to $+16.35\pp$ over the best single mode while spending 1.6--35.3\%
less, in \textbf{8 of 8} cells spanning two benchmarks. The upper bound
$+16.35\pp$ is \cell{wa\_red$\cdot$B0}; restricted to the six VisualWebArena
cells the upper bound is $+16.07\pp$ (\cell{cls$\cdot$B0}). Both figures are
correct and they index different cell sets, so neither may be quoted without
saying which.

The cost range needs its own note, because it moved during this work and the
reason is instructive. ``Best single mode'' is ambiguous when two fixed modes tie
on success, and two cells do: \cell{cls$\cdot$B2} (\mSoM{} and \mVis{} both at
2.23\%) and \cell{wa\_red$\cdot$B1} (\mDOM{}, \mPT{} and \mPP{} all at
16.35\%). Breaking those ties by the mode list's order selects the
\emph{dearer} tied mode, which flatters the oracle's cost advantage: on
\cell{cls$\cdot$B2} it reports $-23.4$\% where a cost-aware tie-break gives
$-1.6$\%. We report the cost-aware figures throughout. \textbf{The range is
therefore 1.6--35.3\%, not the 13.7--35.3\% a list-order comparator would
produce.} The success side is unaffected --- tied modes have equal success by
definition --- and the direction still holds in 8 of 8 cells.

\textbf{Two qualifications travel with this number for the rest of the
dissertation, and they are not cosmetic.} Adding a single \emph{rerun} arm of an
unchanged mode buys 2.0--7.6\pp{} by itself
(Section~\ref{sec:noise-floor}). And at matched arm count, six reruns of the
strongest single mode would reach within 3.53\pp{} of the six-mode oracle ---
below our own noise threshold (Section~\ref{sec:six-vs-six}). What survives is
not that representation selection reaches a \emph{higher} ceiling than
repetition, but that it reaches a comparable one at roughly a sixth of the
serving cost.
\end{scopebox}

\subsection{Where the ceiling comes from}

The oracle decomposes into the two halves defined in
Section~\ref{sec:router-formulation}, and they behave very differently.

\emph{Triage} --- keep the best-SR mode, but spend the cheapest on tasks that
nothing will solve --- delivers 9.5--30.6\% cost reduction at exactly zero
success change in all 8 cells. The zero is by construction, not a discovery: the
policy only redirects tasks that no mode solves, so no success can be lost.
Its interest lies elsewhere: its label is binary, defined on every task, and
therefore \emph{never supply-constrained}. Chapter~\ref{ch:pred} tests exactly
this half, which is the most favourable version of the problem we can pose.

\emph{Which-mode} --- choose among the modes that do solve a task --- supplies
the entire success gain. Its label exists only on solved tasks: 97 of 224 on
\cell{cls$\cdot$B0}, and 15 on \cell{red$\cdot$B2}. That asymmetry is the seed
of Chapter~\ref{ch:disc}'s mechanism.

The full policy ladder per cell --- every fixed mode, both oracle halves, the
cost-tie-broken oracle and the cascade --- is given in
Appendix~\ref{app:ceiling}, which also isolates how much of the oracle's cost
advantage comes from the tie-break rule alone.

\subsection{Two things the ceiling is not}

It is not a policy. It selects using $s_i^{(m)}$ for modes that were never run
on task $i$, which no deployment can observe.

It is also not free of its own label noise, and the amount is measurable rather
than rhetorical. Section~\ref{sec:noise-floor} measures 12.05--14.29\%
task-level discordance between two runs of an \emph{identical} condition, from
which Section~\ref{sec:six-vs-six} estimates that roughly 24\% of
\cell{cls$\cdot$B0}'s tasks are flippable for a single mode. The oracle's
``this mode solves this task'' labels inherit that instability, so a live rerun
would not reproduce its choices task for task. Its \emph{triage} result is where
this bites hardest: the ``zero success loss'' is zero on \emph{this} log,
because the tasks it down-routes are the ones that failed in this run --- not the
ones that fail in expectation.

\begin{scopebox}
\textbf{This is a downgraded claim, and the downgrade is ours.} An earlier
reading of these data --- reported in our own project record --- described the
phantom modes as forming a structurally independent routing region, on the basis
that their drop-one contributions were consistently positive. Consistency of
\emph{direction} survives: 14 of 18 phantom arm--cell values are positive. But
the pooled two-axis effects are $1.35\pm0.32$\pp{} and $2.09\pm0.43$\pp{}
(fixed-effect pool over the six cells). Those sit four to five standard errors
from zero --- so the structure is not a null result --- and still below the most
permissive repetition floor we measure (2.0\pp). Only 1 of 36 drop-one values clears the exact one-sided cutoff at which a single

does not exceed the repeated-sampling floor.} It is not advanced as a positive
structural claim.

\section{The floor: what does running the same thing twice buy?}
\label{sec:noise-floor}

The measurements above are differences between modes. To interpret them we need
to know how large a difference appears between two runs of the \emph{same}
mode. We therefore replicated identical conditions and compared their solve
sets.

\subsection{Measured discordance}

Three arms of \cell{cls$\cdot$B0} have been run twice under an unchanged
configuration, plus one WebArena pilot/full overlap
(Table~\ref{tab:noise-floor}).

Two runs of an identical condition disagree on 12--14\% of tasks. The agent's
decoding is greedy, so this is not sampling stochasticity; it is environment
drift, session state and timing. We had previously recorded the belief that
greedy decoding made local backbones bit-identical across reruns. Step-level
determinism is real and was verified; \emph{episode}-level determinism does not
follow, because an episode also carries site state, wall-clock and session
lifetime. Table~\ref{tab:noise-floor} refutes the stronger belief, and it was
our own.

\subsection{One draw is not a bound}
\label{sec:floor-spread}

The measured $|\Delta\mathrm{SR}|$ between two runs of one condition spans
0.89--2.23\pp. It is tempting to call that the noise floor and declare anything
larger to be signal. That reasoning compares a draw to a draw.

Under the exchangeability null --- the two runs are the same condition, so each
discordant task flips either way with probability $\tfrac12$ --- the difference
is $D=(2X-d)/n$ with $X\sim\mathrm{Binom}(d,\tfrac12)$, giving
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SD}(D)=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n},
  \label{eq:floor-sd}
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the discordant count. Table~\ref{tab:floor-spread} evaluates it.

\emph{could} deliver, not only what it did.} The observed $|\Delta$SR$|$ column

column. The one-sided 95\% point is the threshold an effect must reach before a
single rerun would be unlikely to produce it alone.}
\label{tab:floor-spread}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrrr@{}}
\toprule

  & 95\% (normal) & \textbf{95\% (exact)} \\
\midrule

The upper edge of the observed band (2.23\pp) is the same order as one standard
deviation (2.32--2.53\pp). \textbf{Clearing the band is therefore not clearing
the noise.}

How much further an effect must reach depends on whether the null is evaluated
exactly or approximated, and the difference is not cosmetic. Multiplying
$\mathrm{SD}(D)$ by 1.645 gives 3.82--4.15\pp{} --- but $D$ is a lattice
variable on a binomial support, not a normal one, and at these $d$ the
approximation is optimistic. Evaluating $X\sim\mathrm{Binom}(d,\tfrac12)$
exactly gives one-sided 95\% cutoffs of \textbf{4.02, 4.02 and 4.46\pp} for
$d=27,29,32$. We use the exact values. Of the 36 drop-one values in
Table~\ref{tab:dropone}, three clear the normal approximation and only
\textbf{one} clears the exact cutoff for its own arm.

Both figures are themselves lower bounds, since Equation~\ref{eq:floor-sd}
assumes only exchangeability and does not model the one-directional environment
drift we also observe. Appendix~\ref{app:noise} gives the derivation, separates
the two distinct floor quantities this literature tends to conflate, and states
which comparisons each one licenses.

\subsection{The like-for-like comparison}
\label{sec:one-arm-margin}

The honest test is arm-count matched. Starting from one mode, what does adding
\emph{one} more arm buy --- if that arm is a different representation, versus if
it is a rerun of the same representation? Both are
$|\{\text{added}\}\setminus\{\text{baseline}\}|/n$, the same functional at the
same arm count on the same tasks.

On \cell{cls$\cdot$B0}, starting from \mSoM{} at 27.23\%: adding the best
distinct representation (\mDOM{}) buys $7.14\pp$; adding a rerun buys
$4.91$--$7.59\pp$. The representation arm is \emph{inside} the rerun band.

  the rerun band. The formal relationship to \mbox{pass@$k$}
  \citep{kulal2019spoc, chen2021codex} is direct: adding a rerun arm is
  \mbox{pass@2} of one mode, and the question is whether a second
  \emph{representation} outperforms a second \emph{attempt}.}
  \label{fig:f10b}
\end{figure}

\begin{scopebox}
\textbf{What the one-arm result licenses.} \emph{Licensed} on
\cell{cls$\cdot$B0}: at the one-arm margin, a distinct representation is worth
no more than a rerun of the same representation. \emph{Not licensed} from this
comparison alone: ``the whole six-mode ceiling is noise''. The ceiling adds
\emph{five} arms; we hold \emph{one} rerun arm, and reruns have their own
diminishing returns, so the subtraction is not available. The next section asks
the six-arm question directly instead of declining it.
\end{scopebox}

\section{Six reruns versus six modes}
\label{sec:six-vs-six}

Refusing an invalid subtraction is not the same as answering the question behind
it. If one extra arm of repetition buys up to 7.6\pp, what would \emph{five}
buy --- and is the six-mode ceiling distinguishable from six repetitions of the
strongest single mode? We cannot measure this directly, since that would need six
replicate runs of one condition. We can bound it.

Adopt the same exchangeability null already used in
Section~\ref{sec:floor-spread}, and nothing further. Partition the scored set,
for a given mode, into $s_0$ tasks it solves deterministically, $m$ tasks it
solves with probability $\tfrac12$, and the rest it never solves. Two runs then
disagree on each flippable task with probability $\tfrac12$, so the observed
discordant count $d$ estimates $m/2$, giving $\hat m = 2d$ and
$\hat s_0 = |A| - d$. The union over $k$ independent runs --- which is
$\mathrm{pass}@k$ for that one arm --- is
\begin{equation}
  U(k) = s_0 + m\left(1 - 2^{-k}\right).
  \label{eq:union}
\end{equation}

$U(1)$ reproduces the single-run success rate by construction, so it is not
evidence. $U(2)$ is not: it can be checked against the \emph{observed} two-run
union, and Table~\ref{tab:six-vs-six} reports both.

\centering\small

Six reruns of \mSoM{} would reach 39.77\%; six distinct modes reach 43.30\%.
The residual is \textbf{3.53\pp}.

\begin{scopebox}
\textbf{This is the strongest attack on our headline, and it substantially
lands.} $3.53$\pp{} sits \emph{below} the threshold this dissertation derives in
Section~\ref{sec:floor-spread} and applies to everyone else's effects --- below
it under the normal approximation ($3.82$--$4.15$\pp), and further below it
under the exact binomial null ($4.02$--$4.46$\pp) that we adopt. Applying our own standard to our own headline: \textbf{at
matched arm count, six distinct representations are not distinguishable from six
repetitions of the strongest one.} Most of the ceiling is repetition.

\textbf{What survives is a different claim, on a different axis.} The two are not
interchangeable at deployment. The mode oracle spends \emph{one} episode per
task --- it selects a representation, it does not retry. The six-rerun union
spends \emph{six}. So representation selection reaches a comparable ceiling at
roughly one-sixth of the serving cost. The defensible headline is therefore
about the \emph{cost-efficiency} of the ceiling, not its height.
\end{scopebox}

Two limits on this analysis, both of which cut against reading it too strongly in
either direction. It rests on one cell, because \cell{cls$\cdot$B0} is the only
cell with replicated arms; and Equation~\ref{eq:union} is a two-parameter
caricature of a heterogeneous failure distribution, whose only defence is that it
is checked at $k=2$ and is biased towards the conclusion we did not want.

\section{What the WebArena cells add, and what they do not}
\label{sec:wa-scope}

The two WebArena cells extend the ceiling result to a second benchmark and, in
\cell{wa\_red$\cdot$B0}, produce its largest value ($+16.35\pp$). They also
show the mode ordering rearranging: the best single mode there is \mPT{}
(35.58\%), an image-free mode, with \mSoM{} fourth. That is consistent with the
corpus difference in Figure~\ref{fig:f4}(B) --- but consistency is not
attribution, and we make the weaker claim
(Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks}).

The WebArena rerun figure in Table~\ref{tab:noise-floor} must be read with more
care than the others, and we flag it rather than averaging it in. It pools five
mode-specific pilot-versus-full comparisons over the same 10 registered pilot
tasks, giving 50 panels over ten distinct tasks, while the distinct-arm figure
beside it is computed on 104 tasks starting from \mDOM{} --- whose own
pilot-versus-full comparison shows zero flips in either direction. The 2--4\pp{}
band is generated entirely by \mVis{} and \mPP{}. It should be read as
\emph{some arms of this cell move by 2--4\pp{} under repetition}, not as a floor
for \mDOM{}.

\section{What this chapter establishes}
\label{sec:ch4-summary}

Three findings, in descending order of strength.

\textbf{Strong.} A retrospective oracle over the six modes gains
$+3.45$ to $+16.35\pp$ at 1.6--35.3\% lower cost, in 8 of 8 cells across two
benchmarks, three backbones and two model families. The direction is not in
doubt.

\textbf{Qualified, and the qualification is severe.} At the one-arm margin a
distinct representation is worth no more than a rerun of the same one; at six
arms, repetition of the strongest mode closes to within 3.53\pp{} of the
six-mode oracle, which is below the threshold this chapter derived. The height of
the ceiling is largely a repeated-sampling effect. Its \emph{cost} is not: the
mode oracle reaches that height for one episode per task where repetition needs
six. The surviving claim is about the price of the ceiling, not its height.

\textbf{Downgraded.} The three phantom modes contribute in a consistent
direction (14 of 18 arm--cell values positive) but at a magnitude below the
repetition floor. We report the structure and decline the structural claim.

\chapterhandoff{The fixed-mode study establishes that retrospective selection
has non-trivial headroom, and that a meaningful share of that headroom is
purchasable by repetition rather than by representation. But an oracle chooses
with knowledge of outcomes no deployed system can observe. The operational
question is whether the need for a different representation leaves a detectable
signature \emph{before} the expensive choice is made. Chapter~\ref{ch:pred}
formulates that prediction problem and tests it under a protocol designed so
that a negative answer cannot be blamed on leakage, and a positive answer
cannot be manufactured by the threshold sweep.}
