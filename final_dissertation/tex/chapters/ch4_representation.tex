\chapter{Representation Value: How Much Is There to Route?}
\label{ch:repr}

\chapterhandoff{Chapter~\ref{ch:system} fixed a boundary in which representation
is the only thing that varies. This chapter asks whether that variation is
worth anything. Do the modes succeed on different tasks? How much would a
chooser be worth that was told the answers afterwards? And then the question
that turns out to decide the matter: how much of that is worth more than
simply running one mode twice?}

\FloatBarrier
\section{Cost does not track success}
\label{sec:cost-frontier}

If the expensive modes bought success in proportion to their price there would be nothing to optimise, since one would simply pay for the best affordable mode.
Figure~\ref{fig:f7} shows they do not. Within \cell{cls$\cdot$B0}, \mVis{} is
the cheapest mode at \$0.0648/episode and reaches 25.00\%, while \mPS{} costs
11.2\% more and reaches 15.62\%. Several modes are strictly dominated: they cost
more than an alternative and succeed less.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_f7_cost_sr_frontier.pdf}
  \caption[Cost--success frontier per cell]{%
  \textbf{Cost against success, one panel per cell.} Non-dominated modes are
  drawn solid and joined by a line; dominated modes, which cost more
  \emph{and} succeed less than some alternative, are drawn faded rather than
  hidden. Cost is \texttt{total\_billed\_cost\_usd} per episode throughout, but
  the schedule behind it differs in kind: a vendor billing rate for B0 and a
  device-amortisation rate for B1 and B2. Neither is measured electricity, and
  no panel shares an x axis with another, so the ratio across that boundary is
  never invited (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}). Cost is summed over the steps an
  episode actually took, which is why a weaker mode can be the more expensive
  one: it spends the whole step budget failing. Seven of eight cells carry two
  or three non-dominated modes, which is the premise routing depends on. The
  exception is instructive and is discussed below.}
  \label{fig:f7}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{One cell has a single non-dominated mode, and it is the cell that
matters most later.} On \cell{cls$\cdot$B2}, \mVis{} ties \mSoM{} on success
(2.23\%) while costing 22.1\% less, and beats every other mode on both axes. The
frontier collapses to one point. That is what a regime looks like when
always-cheapest is not merely competitive but dominant, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:e2e} shows it is the general shape of the problem rather than a
quirk of one cell.

\paragraph{The price ordering is not stable across cells either.} \mVis{} is
cheapest in seven of eight cells but not on \cell{wa\_red$\cdot$B0}, where
\mDOM{} is cheapest and \mVis{} is the fourth most expensive of six. And the
spread between cheapest and dearest ranges from 11.6\% (\cell{cls$\cdot$B0}) to
77.8\% (\cell{wa\_red$\cdot$B1}), so ``the cheap mode'' and ``the expensive
mode'' name different amounts of money in different cells. Cost is an outcome of
the interaction, not a property of the representation, which is why every cost figure here is measured per episode rather than assumed from a
context-length calculation.

\FloatBarrier
\section{The modes solve different tasks}
\label{sec:complementarity}

Success rates are marginals; routing needs the joint. Across
\cell{cls$\cdot$B0}, 97 of 224 tasks are solved by at least one mode, and 68 of those by more than one, so 29 tasks are solved by exactly one mode and lost
entirely if that mode is not in the portfolio. The equivalent counts for the
other cells appear in Table~\ref{tab:oracle}; the share of tasks with more than
one solver ranges from 1.5\% (\cell{red$\cdot$B2}) to 34.6\%
(\cell{wa\_red$\cdot$B0}).

The proportion of tasks that \emph{nothing} solves is the more consequential
number, and it is large: 48.1\% on the most capable cell and 92.9\% on the
weakest. Most of the benchmark is out of reach of every mode, and any policy's
room for manoeuvre is confined to the remainder.

There is a second way to ask whether the sides are complementary, and it gives a
weaker answer than the overlap counts do. \mSoM{} is the only mode carrying both
a text payload and an image, so the amount by which it beats each single side
measures what putting the two together is worth. Taking those differences from
Table~\ref{tab:sr-per-mode}: the premium over \mVis{} is positive on six of the
eight cells, exactly zero on \cell{cls$\cdot$B2} where the two tie at 2.23\%,
and negative on \cell{red$\cdot$B2}. The premium over \mDOM{} is negative on
three cells, \cell{red$\cdot$B2} and \emph{both} WebArena cells, where the
plain accessibility tree beats the bundle by 2.89 and 4.80\pp. Adding the
screenshot to the text is therefore not a free improvement even before its price
is counted, and the benchmark on which nothing was tuned is where it fails.

These are directions, and this section deliberately stops short of calling any
of them an effect. Only \cell{cls$\cdot$B0} carries replicated arms, so only
\cell{cls$\cdot$B0} has a measured threshold, and its arm-specific values run
from 3.52 to 4.15\pp{} (Section~\ref{sec:floor-spread}). The \mSoM{} premium
over \mVis{} on that cell is $+2.23$\pp, below even the lowest of the six. For
the remaining seven cells no threshold was measured, and Appendix~\ref{app:noise}
forbids borrowing one from a neighbouring cell, so their premiums are reported
and not tested.

\begin{scopebox}
\textbf{Headline, with its comparator and its cell set attached.} A
retrospective oracle over six observation modes improves task success by
$+3.45$ to $+16.35\pp$ over the best single mode while spending 1.6--35.3\%
less, in \textbf{8 of 8} cells spanning two benchmarks. The upper bound
$+16.35\pp$ is \cell{wa\_red$\cdot$B0}; restricted to the six VisualWebArena
cells the upper bound is $+16.07\pp$ (\cell{cls$\cdot$B0}). Both figures are
correct and they index different cell sets, so neither may be quoted without
saying which.

The cost range needs its own note, because it moved during this work and the
reason is instructive. ``Best single mode'' is ambiguous when two fixed modes tie
on success, and two cells do: \cell{cls$\cdot$B2} (\mSoM{} and \mVis{} both at
2.23\%) and \cell{wa\_red$\cdot$B1} (\mDOM{}, \mPT{} and \mPP{} all at
16.35\%). Breaking those ties by the mode list's order selects the
\emph{dearer} tied mode, which flatters the oracle's cost advantage: on
\cell{cls$\cdot$B2} it reports $-23.4$\% where a cost-aware tie-break gives
$-1.6$\%. We report the cost-aware figures throughout. \textbf{The range is
therefore 1.6--35.3\%, not the 13.7--35.3\% a list-order comparator would
produce.} The success side is unaffected, since tied modes have equal success by definition, and the direction still holds in 8 of 8 cells.

\textbf{This number travels with two qualifications for the rest of the dissertation, and they are not cosmetic.} Adding a single \emph{rerun} arm of an
unchanged mode buys 2.0--7.6\pp{} by itself
(Section~\ref{sec:noise-floor}). And at matched arm count, six reruns of the
strongest single mode take more than half the distance to the six-mode oracle
however often tasks flip, and all but 3.53\pp{} of it, which is below our own
noise threshold, if they flip like fair coins (Section~\ref{sec:six-vs-six}). What survives is
not that representation selection reaches a \emph{higher} ceiling than
repetition, but that it reaches a comparable one at roughly a sixth of the
serving cost.
\end{scopebox}

\subsection{Where the ceiling comes from}

The oracle decomposes into the two halves defined in
Section~\ref{sec:router-formulation}, and they behave very differently.

\emph{Triage}, which keeps the best-SR mode but spends the cheapest on tasks that nothing will solve, reduces cost by up to 30.6\% at exactly zero success
change. The zero is by construction, not a discovery: the policy only redirects
tasks that no mode solves, so no success can be lost.

\begin{scopebox}
\textbf{The same tie-break correction applies here, and it costs one cell.}
Measured against the list-order ``best single mode'', triage saves 9.5--30.6\%
in 8 of 8 cells. Against the \emph{cost-aware} tied best single
(Section~\ref{sec:ceiling}) the range is $-30.6$\% to $+1.1$\%, cheaper in
\textbf{7 of 8}. The cell that flips is \cell{cls$\cdot$B2}, where \mVis{}
matches \mSoM{}'s 2.23\% success for 22.1\% less money: against that comparator
triage is 1.1\% \emph{more} expensive, so its apparent saving there was an
artefact of comparing to a needlessly expensive fixed policy. We quote the
corrected figures. The direction survives in 7 of 8 and the upper end is unchanged, but ``in every cell'' does not survive, and it was the strongest
word in the sentence.
\end{scopebox}
Its interest lies elsewhere: its label is binary, defined on every task, and
therefore \emph{never supply-constrained}. Chapter~\ref{ch:pred} tests exactly
this half, which is the most favourable version of the problem we can pose.

\emph{Which-mode}, choosing among the modes that do solve a task, supplies the entire success gain. Its label exists only on solved tasks: 97 of 224 on
\cell{cls$\cdot$B0}, and 15 on \cell{red$\cdot$B2}. That asymmetry is the seed
of Chapter~\ref{ch:disc}'s mechanism.

The full ladder of policies per cell (every fixed mode, both halves of the
oracle, the version that breaks ties by measured cost, and the policy that
tries modes in increasing order of cost until one succeeds) is given in
Appendix~\ref{app:ceiling}, which also isolates how much of the oracle's cost
advantage comes from the tie-break rule alone.

\subsection{What the ceiling is not}

It is not a policy. For each task it reads off the outcome of every mode,
including modes that were never run on that task, which is information no deployed
system can have.

It is also not free of its own label noise, and the amount is measurable rather
than rhetorical. Section~\ref{sec:noise-floor} measures 12.05--14.29\%
task-level discordance between two runs of an \emph{identical} condition, from
which Section~\ref{sec:six-vs-six} estimates that roughly 24\% of that cell's
tasks are \emph{flippable} for a single mode: they can come out either way on
two runs of the same setup, with nothing changed between them. The oracle's
``this mode solves this task'' labels inherit that instability, so a live rerun
would not reproduce its choices task for task. Its \emph{triage} result is where
this bites hardest: the ``zero success loss'' is zero on \emph{this} log only,
because the tasks it sends to the cheap mode are the ones that happened to
fail in this run, not the ones that would fail on average.

\begin{scopebox}
\textbf{This is a downgraded claim, and the downgrade is ours.} An earlier reading of these data, reported in our own project record, described the phantom modes as forming a structurally independent routing region, on the basis
that their drop-one contributions were consistently positive. Consistency of
\emph{direction} survives: 14 of 18 phantom arm--cell values are positive. But
the two axes these modes separate, which page text is supplied and how the
instructions are worded, pool to effects of only $1.35\pm0.32$\pp{} and
$2.09\pm0.43$\pp{} (fixed-effect pool over the six cells). Those sit four to five standard errors from zero, so the structure is not a null result, and still below the most permissive repetition floor we measure (2.0\pp). Only 1 of 36 drop-one values clears the exact one-sided cutoff at which a single
rerun would be unlikely to produce the effect on its own; three do under the looser normal approximation, which is why Section~\ref{sec:floor-spread} adopts
the exact null. Precision is not the problem; magnitude is. \textbf{The correct statement is therefore: we
observe a directionally consistent complementarity structure whose magnitude
does not exceed the repeated-sampling floor.} It is not advanced as a positive
structural claim.
\end{scopebox}

style; the larger unique contributions come from \emph{crossing sides}: \mSoM{} and \mVis{}, the only modes that send an image. Grouping the
modes by what is actually transmitted turns an otherwise puzzling pattern into
an expected one.

\section{What running the same thing twice buys}
\label{sec:noise-floor}

The measurements above are differences between modes. To interpret them we need
to know how large a difference appears between two runs of the \emph{same}
mode. We therefore replicated identical conditions and compared their solve
sets.

Two runs of an identical condition disagree on 10--14\% of tasks. The agent's
decoding is greedy, so this is not sampling stochasticity; it is environment
drift, session state and timing. We had previously recorded the belief that
greedy decoding made local backbones bit-identical across reruns. Step-level
determinism is real and was verified; \emph{episode}-level determinism does not
follow, because an episode also depends on site state, wall-clock and session lifetime. Table~\ref{tab:noise-floor} refutes the stronger belief, and it was
our own.

\subsection{One draw is not a bound}
\label{sec:floor-spread}

The measured $|\Delta\mathrm{SR}|$ between two runs of one condition spans
0.89--2.68\pp. It is tempting to call that the noise floor and declare anything
larger to be signal. That reasoning compares a draw to a draw.

Under the exchangeability null, where the two runs are the same condition and each discordant task flips either way with probability $\tfrac12$, the difference is $D=(2X-d)/n$ with $X\sim\mathrm{Binom}(d,\tfrac12)$, giving
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SD}(D)=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n},
  \label{eq:floor-sd}
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the discordant count. Table~\ref{tab:floor-spread} evaluates it.

How much further an effect must reach depends on whether the null is evaluated
exactly or approximated, and the difference is not cosmetic. Multiplying
$\mathrm{SD}(D)$ by 1.645 gives 3.52--4.15\pp, but $D$ can only take a
discrete set of values, fixed by how many tasks the two runs disagree on,
rather than varying continuously. Evaluating $X\sim\mathrm{Binom}(d,\tfrac12)$
exactly gives one-sided 95\% cutoffs of \textbf{3.12, 3.57, 4.02, 4.02, 4.02
and 4.46\pp} for $d=23,28,27,27,29,32$. We use the exact values. Which of the

with $d\ge27$ the exact cutoff is the higher one, and for $d=23$ and $d=28$ it
is the lower. Of the 36 drop-one values in
Table~\ref{tab:dropone}, three clear the normal approximation and only
\textbf{one} clears the exact cutoff for its own arm.

assumes only exchangeability and does not model the one-directional environment
drift we also observe. Appendix~\ref{app:noise} gives the derivation, separates
the two distinct floor quantities this literature tends to conflate, and states
which comparisons each one licenses.

\subsection{The like-for-like comparison}
\label{sec:one-arm-margin}

The honest test is arm-count matched. Starting from one mode, what does adding \emph{one} more arm buy, if that arm is a different representation rather than a rerun of the same representation? Both are
$|\{\text{added}\}\setminus\{\text{baseline}\}|/n$, the same functional at the
same arm count on the same tasks.

On \cell{cls$\cdot$B0}, starting from \mSoM{} at 27.23\%: adding the best
distinct representation (\mDOM{}) buys $7.14\pp$; adding a rerun buys
$4.46$--$7.59\pp$. The representation arm is \emph{inside} the rerun band.

\textbf{What the one-arm result licenses.} \emph{Licensed} on

comparison alone: ``the whole six-mode ceiling is noise''. The ceiling adds
\emph{five} arms; we hold \emph{one} rerun arm, and reruns have their own
diminishing returns, so the subtraction is not available. The next section asks
the six-arm question directly instead of declining it.
\end{scopebox}

\FloatBarrier
\section{Six reruns versus six modes}
\label{sec:six-vs-six}

Refusing an invalid subtraction is not the same as answering the question behind
it. If one extra arm of repetition buys up to 7.6\pp, what would \emph{five} buy, and is the six-mode ceiling distinguishable from six repetitions of the
strongest single mode? We cannot measure this directly, since that would need six
replicate runs of one condition. We can project it, within limits the two runs
we do have are able to set.

Adopt the same exchangeability null already used in
Section~\ref{sec:floor-spread}, and nothing further. Partition the scored set, for one mode, into $s_0$ tasks it solves deterministically, $m$ tasks it
solves with probability $p$, and the rest it never solves. The union over $k$
independent runs, which is $\mathrm{pass}@k$ for that one arm, is
\begin{equation}
  U(k) = s_0 + m\left(1 - (1-p)^{k}\right).
  \label{eq:union}
\end{equation}

Two runs constrain this through the single-run count $|A| = s_0 + mp$ and the
expected discordant count $\mathbb{E}[d] = 2mp(1-p)$. Those two observables do
not identify $p$; they identify a one-parameter family along which $U(k)$
moves. Writing $q = 1-p$, the family collapses to
$U(k) = |A| + \tfrac{d}{2}\left(1 + q + \dots + q^{k-2}\right)$, which is
\emph{decreasing} in $p$ and, at $k=2$, independent of it altogether.
Appendix~\ref{sec:app-union} derives both. Two consequences matter here. Fixing
$p=\tfrac12$, which is what $\hat m = 2d$, $\hat s_0 = |A|-d$ amounts to,
does not yield an upper bound; it picks one point low in the range. And the
$k=2$ comparison cannot test that choice, because every $p$ predicts the same
$U(2)$. We therefore report the whole interval the feasibility constraints
$s_0 \ge 0$ and $s_0 + m \le n$ admit, and draw only the conclusion that holds
across it.

Six reruns of \mSoM{} would reach 39.77\% if every flippable task were a fair
coin, and somewhere in $[35.72\%, 54.33\%]$ over the range of $p$ the two runs
admit; six distinct modes reach 43.30\%. At the fair-coin point the residual is
\textbf{3.53\pp}, but the interval straddles the oracle, so the comparison
cannot be settled at a point. What the interval does settle is the \emph{share}:
across its entire width, six reruns recover between \textbf{52.8\%} and
\textbf{168.6\%} of the 16.07\pp{} of headroom between \mSoM{} and the
six-mode oracle. Both endpoints are plug-in quantities computed at the observed
moments, not confidence bounds: a paired-task bootstrap over the 224 tasks
leaves the lower one above one half in only about \textbf{59\%} of resamples.
``Repetition takes most of the ceiling'' is therefore a model-based sensitivity,
not something two runs of one arm establish.

\begin{scopebox}
\textbf{This is the strongest attack on our headline, and it substantially
lands.} At the fair-coin point, $3.53$\pp{} sits \emph{below} the threshold this
dissertation derives in Section~\ref{sec:floor-spread} and applies to everyone
else's effects. The comparison is against \mSoM{}'s own row, since \mSoM{} is
the arm the projection is about: $3.95$\pp{} under the normal approximation and
$4.02$\pp{} under the exact binomial null we adopt. Judging it against the
lowest threshold in the table instead would let the verdict depend on which
\emph{other} modes happened to be replicated, which is not a property any
conclusion should have, and it is the same arm-matched rule
Section~\ref{sec:floor-spread} already applies to the drop-one values.
Applying our own standard to our own headline: \textbf{at
matched arm count, six distinct representations are not distinguishable from six
repetitions of the strongest one.} Most of the ceiling is repetition.

\textbf{What survives is a different claim, on a different axis.} The two are not
interchangeable at deployment. The mode oracle spends \emph{one} episode per task, selecting a representation rather than retrying. The six-rerun union
spends \emph{six}. So representation selection reaches a comparable ceiling at
roughly one-sixth of the serving cost. The defensible headline is therefore
about the \emph{cost-efficiency} of the ceiling, not its height.
\end{scopebox}

The analysis has two limits, both of which cut against reading it too strongly in either direction. It rests on one cell, because \cell{cls$\cdot$B0} is the only
cell with replicated arms, though all six of its arms now have one; and
Equation~\ref{eq:union} compresses a whole distribution of task-level success
probabilities into a two-parameter family that two runs constrain but cannot pin
down. That is why the result is reported as an interval, and why the claim drawn
from it is the one that holds across the interval's whole width. Letting each
task carry its own $p_i$ does not widen it: optimising $U(6)$ over arbitrary
$\{p_i\}$ under the same two moment constraints reaches the same endpoints,
because the feasibility constraints, not the homogeneity assumption, are what
bind.

\section{What the WebArena cells add, and what they do not}
\label{sec:wa-scope}

The two WebArena cells extend the ceiling result to a second benchmark and, in
\cell{wa\_red$\cdot$B0}, produce its largest value ($+16.35\pp$). They also
show the mode ordering rearranging: the best single mode there is \mPT{}
(35.58\%), an image-free mode, with \mSoM{} fourth. That is consistent with the
corpus difference in Figure~\ref{fig:f4}(B), but consistency is not attribution, and we make the weaker claim
(Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks}).

The WebArena rerun figure in Table~\ref{tab:noise-floor} must be read with more
care than the others, and we flag it rather than averaging it in. It pools
five modes' pilot-versus-full comparisons over the same 10 registered pilot
tasks, so its 50 comparisons come from only ten distinct tasks, while the distinct-arm figure
beside it is computed on 104 tasks starting from \mDOM{}, whose own pilot-versus-full comparison shows zero flips in either direction. The 2--4\pp{}
band is generated entirely by \mVis{} and \mPP{}. It should be read as
\emph{some arms of this cell move by 2--4\pp{} under repetition}, not as a floor
for \mDOM{}.

\section{What this chapter establishes}
\label{sec:ch4-summary}

Three findings, in descending order of strength.

\textbf{Strong.} A retrospective oracle over the six modes gains
$+3.45$ to $+16.35\pp$ at 1.6--35.3\% lower cost, in 8 of 8 cells across two
benchmarks, three backbones and two model families. The direction is not in
doubt.

\textbf{Qualified, and the qualification is severe.} If only one extra run is
allowed, a different representation is worth no more than a repeat of the same
one. At the matched arm count of six, and under a fair-coin model of which
tasks flip, repeating the strongest mode comes
within 3.53\pp{} of what six different modes achieve, which is below the
threshold this chapter derived. The two sides are matched in arms, not in what
they cost to run: the mode oracle reaches its level with one episode per task,
where repetition needs six. The height of
the ceiling is largely a repeated-sampling effect. Its \emph{cost} is not: the
mode oracle reaches that height for one episode per task where repetition needs
six. The surviving claim is about the price of the ceiling, not its height.

\textbf{Downgraded.} The three phantom modes contribute in a consistent
direction (14 of 18 arm--cell values positive) but at a magnitude below the
repetition floor. We report the structure and decline the structural claim.

\chapterhandoff{Comparing the six fixed modes establishes that there is real
room above the best single one, and that a meaningful share of that room can
be bought by repetition instead of by a different representation. But the
chooser that reaches it is told which modes worked, which no deployed system
can know. The operational question is whether the need for a different
representation shows up in anything observable \emph{before} the expensive
choice is made. Chapter~\ref{ch:pred}
formulates that prediction problem and tests it under a protocol designed so
that a negative answer cannot be blamed on test information having leaked into
training, and a positive answer cannot be produced by trying decision
thresholds until one works.}
