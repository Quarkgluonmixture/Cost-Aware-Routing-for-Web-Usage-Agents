\chapter{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{ch:disc}

\chapterhandoff{Chapters~\ref{ch:repr}--\ref{ch:e2e} established a ceiling, a
failure to predict, and a failure to profit. This chapter explains the second of
those in terms of a mechanism, states the regime in which all three hold, and
answers the research question at exactly the level of evidence available.}

\section{Why the prediction failed: a supply constraint}
\label{sec:mechanism}

The natural explanations for a failed classifier are the hypothesis class, the
feature set, or the label definition. On this data none of them is the binding
constraint. The binding constraint is that \textbf{routing labels are produced
only by solve events, and solve events are scarce}.

Consider how the examples dwindle. Take the
classified-ads site with the 235B model (\cell{cls$\cdot$B0}), the most
favourable cell in the study. Of its 224 scored tasks, some mode solves 97.
Only those 97 can say anything about which mode to prefer, so there are 97
labels. Spread across six possible answers, only three of the six retain
enough examples to be split five ways for training and testing. On the forum
site with the Gemma model (\cell{red$\cdot$B2}), 203 tasks produce 15 labels,
and no answer retains enough at all.
Pooled over the six VisualWebArena cells there are 260 which-mode labels in
total.


The shortage is in how often tasks are solved, not in how the resulting
examples are divided up. Dividing the examples we have differently cannot
create a solved task that never happened. Three
independent routes out of the constraint are available in principle; we
attempted all three, and each is blocked for a structurally different reason.

\begin{scopebox}
\textbf{But ``structural'' should not be heard as ``insurmountable'', and our own
arithmetic sets the scale.} Labels are minted at the solvable rate, so the
benchmark size needed to put two classes above the admissibility threshold is
computable. It is $0.5\times$ the current corpus for \cell{cls$\cdot$B0},
$0.9\times$ for \cell{cls$\cdot$B1}, and $2.1$--$4.2\times$ for the four cells that currently fail, at most 846 reddit tasks against the 203 we score
(Section~\ref{sec:undersampling}). That is a benchmark somebody could build, not
an impossibility. \textbf{The honest claim is therefore that the which-mode
half cannot be fitted on the number of solved tasks \emph{the benchmarks that
exist supply}, and that the increase required is a small multiple rather than
an order of magnitude.} This is
weaker than an impossibility result and more useful than one: it is a
specification.

The arithmetic carries one assumption worth naming, because it is the assumption
a sceptic should attack first. It holds the \emph{class proportions} fixed while scaling $n$, so new tasks are assumed to distribute their winning modes as the
current ones do. A larger corpus drawn from a different template mix would not,
and the 75 templates behind VisualWebArena classifieds
(Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks}) are a reminder that task diversity grows more
slowly than task count. The multiples above are therefore a lower bound on the
corpus needed, not a promise.
\end{scopebox}

\subsection{Route 1: make the label continuous}

If success were graded rather than binary, every task would be informative, and scarcity would disappear. VisualWebArena's evaluator returns a binary verdict.
Its component checks (matching the web address, inspecting the resulting
page, matching text, and querying the image) each return a pass or a fail,
and the task passes only if every one of them passes. There is no partial
credit to expose. The route is closed by the benchmark's
measurement instrument, not by our analysis.

\subsection{Route 2: pool across cells}

Pooling tasks across backbones multiplies the label count. It also makes the
target ambiguous, because the same task acquires a different correct answer
under a different model. On classifieds, 54 tasks are covered by two or more cells and
\textbf{31 of them (57.4\%) carry conflicting labels}; on reddit the conflict
rate is 56.0\% of 25 shared tasks. A pooled classifier is being asked to predict
a quantity that is not a function of its inputs.

We report the associated agreement ceilings with an explicit caveat that they
are \emph{not} what they may look like. Grouping by task and predicting each
group's most common answer attains 79.2\% (classifieds) and 83.7\% (reddit);
grouping instead by identical input measurements attains more. Neither figure
is a bound on anything. Each is scored on the very rows it read the most
common answer off, so a group containing a single row is counted correct
automatically, and such groups are 29.8\% and 37.0\% of rows respectively.
They measure agreement within the data already seen, not accuracy on tasks not
yet attempted, and we do not use them as bounds.

\subsection{Route 3: relabel to a coarser target}

A coarser question is easier to answer. Instead of asking which of six modes
to use, we can ask only which price band the right mode falls in. Re-slicing
the same inputs that way lifts the agreement figure to 89.9\% on classifieds
and 96.7\% on reddit, without inventing a single new solved task. Both numbers
are the same kind of in-sample figure as the pair above, and carry the same
caveat: they are agreement within data already seen, not accuracy on new
tasks. It is the only relabelling that buys anything, and what it buys is a
coarser decision, not more examples. The
route is open but leads somewhere smaller than where we were going.


\subsection{A defect we found in our own supervision}

Auditing the label revealed a flaw worth recording, because it is a plausible
mistake for anyone building this pipeline. Our canonical which-mode label is
``the first successful mode in a list ordered by prior cost''. The list is
documented as ascending in cost. It is not, against measured cost: on
12.5--54.6\% of labels, depending on cell, the ordering returns a mode that is
strictly \emph{more} expensive than another successful one. The label is
therefore not ``cheapest successful mode''; it is whatever the list reached
first.

We had earlier recorded this as a tie-breaking problem affecting roughly a
quarter of labels. That was the wrong frame and the wrong quantity. Exact ties
occur in \textbf{zero} labels in every cell, since measured cost is a continuous float, and two modes costing exactly the same does not happen. The real defect
is not arbitrary tie-breaking but a false ordering assumption, which is both
more common and worse. Table~\ref{tab:supply} and its sensitivity analysis in
Section~\ref{sec:robustness} are computed under both labels, and the
supply conclusion is unchanged.

\section{Oracle headroom is not learnable headroom}
\label{sec:headroom-gap}

Chapter~\ref{ch:repr} measured a ceiling of $+3.45$ to $+16.35\pp$;
Chapter~\ref{ch:e2e} realised none of it. The gap decomposes into four terms,
each of which we can now attach a measurement to:
% Broken after the product term: on a 13cm text block the four braced factors do
% not fit on one line, and \resizebox would shrink the annotations below the
% figure-label size used everywhere else.
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \underbrace{\text{realised value}}_{\approx 0}
  \;\approx\;
  &\underbrace{\text{selection opportunity}}_{+3.45\ \text{to}\ +16.35\pp}
  \;\times\;
  \underbrace{\text{decision quality}}_{\text{AUROC } 0.53\text{--}0.76} \\[4pt]
  &\;-\;
  \underbrace{\text{routing overhead}}_{\sim10^{-6}\,\$}
  \;-\;
  \underbrace{\text{error cost}}_{\text{dominant}} .
\end{split}
  \label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}

Read the equation as a sentence: what routing actually delivers is the
opportunity that exists, scaled down by how well the choice can be predicted,
minus what the choosing costs and what the wrong choices cost.
Equation~\ref{eq:decomposition} is a conceptual decomposition; we do not
estimate every term jointly. But the ordering of magnitudes is measured, and it
locates the loss unambiguously. The overhead term is negligible
(Section~\ref{sec:overhead}). The opportunity term is the one quantity here that is not in doubt. The
loss is split between two terms. The first is how well the choice can be
predicted at all: weak, and mostly borrowed from a benchmark annotation a
deployment would not have. The second is what the wrong choices cost, and this
dominates, because 48--93\% of tasks are solved by no mode at all, so the
error term is made up mostly of tasks sent to the expensive mode that were
never going to succeed whatever they were shown.

There is also a fifth term, absent from the usual formulation, that this study
was forced to add: \textbf{the opportunity itself is largely repetition}. Adding
one \emph{rerun} arm buys 2.0--7.6\pp{} on its own
(Section~\ref{sec:noise-floor}); if only one extra run is allowed, a different
representation is worth no more than a repeat of the same one
(Figure~\ref{fig:f10b}); and when both sides are given six runs, repeating the
strongest single mode takes more than half of what six different modes add over
it, and, under a fair-coin model of which tasks flip, comes within
3.53\pp{} of matching them (Section~\ref{sec:six-vs-six}). An accounting that treats
the full ceiling as representation-attributable is therefore claiming something
our own data contradicts. What survives is not an advantage in success but an
advantage in \emph{how many attempts it takes}: selection reaches that level
with one attempt per task where repetition needs six. That was always the axis
on which routing was supposed to pay, and which makes the negative result
of Chapter~\ref{ch:e2e} sharper rather than softer.

\section{What the phantom-space result does and does not mean}
\label{sec:phantom-meaning}

The four image-free modes are a clean experimental instrument. Two things vary
independently across them, which page text is supplied and how the
instructions around it are worded, while the image is absent throughout.
And because \mSoM{}'s text is only $1.008\times$ the length of \mDOM{}'s,
removing the image removes almost nothing but the image. As an instrument, it
works.

As a finding, it is weaker than we initially read it. The three image-free
modes other than \mDOM{}, namely \mPT{}, \mPP{} and \mPS{}, which we call the
\emph{phantom} modes, do point the same way: in 14 of 18 mode-by-cell
comparisons, deleting one of them lowers the ceiling. But the pooled sizes
along the two axes, 1.35 and 2.09\pp, sit below even the most permissive floor
we measure for what a plain rerun produces. So we report that the phantom
modes consistently add \emph{some} tasks the others miss, and decline the
stronger claim that they occupy their own region of the space
(Section~\ref{sec:dropone}).

It is fair to ask why a declined claim still gets a section, a table and a
figure. Two reasons, and neither is attachment to the result. The 36 drop-one
values are the evidence \emph{for} the decline. Deleting them would leave the
reader an assertion where there is now a measurement, and leave a later reader
no way to check whether the effect was small or simply absent. And Chapter~\ref{ch:system} depends on the factorisation that produced them, regardless of what it found: it is what licenses
attributing anything at all to the image rather than to the text or the prompt.
What we do not do is let the structure carry weight elsewhere, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:e2e}'s result depends on none of it.

We also decline two adjacent readings that the data does not support. The
image-free modes are not a cheaper Set-of-Marks: \mSoM{} is the strongest single
mode in four of six VisualWebArena cells. And the modes are not evidence that
the model reasons differently inside; they are evidence that different inputs
lead it to succeed on different tasks. A mechanistic reading
would require separate evidence, which we did not collect.

\section{Sustainability: what was measured, and what follows}
\label{sec:sustainability-disc}

For the two models we run on our own hardware, we measured the energy the
graphics card itself reports drawing while it works. For the third, reached
through a paid remote service, we can see the tokens billed but not the
electricity used, so no energy figure exists for it. Taking the UK grid at
220\,g of carbon-dioxide-equivalent emissions per kilowatt-hour (written
CO\textsubscript{2}e), one classifieds task on the 4B Qwen model (B1)
corresponds to roughly 1.1--1.3\,g CO\textsubscript{2}e, and the spread across
the six modes within a cell is under 20\%.

Three things follow, and one prominently does not.

The routing result is \emph{not} an efficiency result. Since no learned policy
is both more successful and cheaper than always using the cheapest mode, this
dissertation produces no compute saving to convert into an environmental
claim, and we make none.

The one measured compute reduction belongs to the after-the-fact chooser
deciding only whether \emph{any} mode will solve the task. It saves up to 30.6\%
of cost at no loss of success, in 7 of 8 cells, measured against a comparator
that already takes cost into account (Section~\ref{sec:ceiling}). But it
cannot be deployed, so it describes an opportunity rather than a saving.

The measurement boundary excludes the host processor, cooling and building
overhead, the compute used by the browser environment itself, the emissions
embodied in manufacturing the hardware, and the entire remote data centre
behind B0. Following \citet{gupta2022chasing} and
\citet{luccioni2022bloom}, the boundary materially determines the number, so
what we report is GPU-device operational energy and a corresponding emissions estimate, not a footprint. And no token reduction anywhere in this document is
converted into an energy reduction, because no hardware-independent conversion
exists \citep{fernandez2025energy}.

\section{The regime this describes}
\label{sec:regime-boundary}

Our conclusions are indexed to a regime with three properties, and the value of a negative result depends entirely on stating them.

\paragraph{Base success 2--36\%.} At these rates most tasks go unsolved
whatever mode is used. Two things follow, and both mechanisms we identify are
consequences of this one number: always using the cheapest mode is close to
the best one can do, and there are very few solved tasks to learn from. A
model succeeding on 60--70\% of tasks would have more examples to learn from,
a smaller share of unsolvable tasks, and a comparator that is no longer nearly
cost-optimal. \textbf{We do not claim the result
transfers there, and we would expect it not to.}

\paragraph{A cost-minimising fixed comparator.} We compare against the cheapest
fixed policy because a deployment has it for free. A study comparing against
the best-performing fixed policy instead would report large routing wins from
the same data, since the after-the-fact chooser is both more successful and
cheaper than the best single mode in all eight cells. The comparator
choice is doing real work, and we consider ours the honest one; a reader who
disagrees can read the same result off Table~\ref{tab:oracle}.

\paragraph{Task-level routing.} Our decision unit is the task, not the step.
Chapter~\ref{ch:system} formulates routing per step
(Equation~\ref{eq:router}), but the labels available are task-level, because
that is where the evaluator's signal is. A step-level router might find
structure a task-level one cannot, and would face an even worse label-supply problem, since a step carries no outcome of its own.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

These are boundaries on the conclusions, ordered by how much they would change
them.

\paragraph{The undersampling objection is answered for the result and conceded
for the predictor.} Section~\ref{sec:undersampling} runs the experiment this
paragraph previously said was outstanding, and it does not come back entirely in
our favour. The learning curves are still rising, and a barely-constrained
model does better than the same model trained on randomly shuffled outcomes in
every cell. So more data would produce a better predictor, and we say so. What
the objection cannot reach is the success-and-cost result, because the best
any predictor could become is the after-the-fact chooser, and that chooser is
both more successful and cheaper than always-cheapest in only 1 of 8 cells. \textbf{The residual limitation is
narrower than the original objection but real}: we have shown that a
\emph{better classifier} would not change the conclusion, not that a
\emph{larger benchmark} would leave everything else fixed. A benchmark four
times the size would also change the base success rate, the share of tasks
nothing solves, and how hard always-cheapest is to beat on cost, and those,
not the number of training rows, are what Section~\ref{sec:regime-boundary}
identifies as the regime.

\paragraph{Replay, not live routing.} Chapter~\ref{ch:e2e}'s policies are
scored on runs that had already been completed: for each task we credit the
policy with the outcome that mode actually produced. This leaves out what the
routing would cost while running, and any chance that an earlier choice of
mode changes which pages the agent then encounters. The direction of the bias
favours the router, so the negative result is conservative, but a live routed run remains the stronger evidence, and we do not have it.

\paragraph{One benchmark family, two sites, three backbones.} Both benchmarks
are self-hosted static replicas of real sites. Live-web instability, a wider
site mix, and larger or differently trained backbones are all outside our
evidence. VisualWebArena shopping was run but excluded
(Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks}), which costs the external-validity argument a
third site it could otherwise have had.

\paragraph{The large model cannot be independently re-run.} B0's conditions
can be replayed from our archived logs, but re-executing them requires the
same paid remote access we had. None of the conclusions rests on that model
alone: the router's 0-of-8 result and the chooser's 8-of-8 improvement over
the best single mode both hold across the other two models as well.

\paragraph{The noise floor is measured on one cell.} Six replicated arms exist
on \cell{cls$\cdot$B0} and one pooled comparison on \cell{wa\_red$\cdot$B1}. The
thresholds in Table~\ref{tab:floor-spread} are computed there and applied
elsewhere. When a third repeat was run, its result landed inside the range the
first two had marked out, which is reassuring but is one confirmation rather than a
distribution. We also cannot construct the six-arm repetition comparison the
ceiling would strictly require, because that needs six replicate runs of one
mode.

\paragraph{One data file carries a misleading quality stamp.} All 36 rows in
the drop-one file are complete and exact, yet every row is stamped
\texttt{NON\_PAPER\_GRADE}, not because anything is missing, but because
the script that wrote it was launched with a flag (\texttt{--allow-partial})
that permits incomplete input. We report the stamp rather than removing it
(Appendix~\ref{app:repro}); the values support only the downgraded claim in
either case.

\section{Future work the evidence actually implies}
\label{sec:future}

\paragraph{Test the regime boundary directly.} The single most informative
follow-up is to re-run the identical protocol on a backbone with 50--70\% base
success. Our mechanism predicts what should then happen: more solved tasks to
learn from, a smaller share of tasks nothing can solve, always-cheapest no
longer close to optimal, and room for a chooser to be both more successful and
cheaper.
If routing still fails there, the mechanism claim is wrong; if it succeeds, the
regime boundary is located.

\paragraph{Build the benchmark the supply arithmetic specifies.} The scale
needed to bring the which-mode half above that minimum in the four failing
cells is $2.1$--$4.2\times$ the current corpus, at most 846 reddit tasks (Section~\ref{sec:undersampling}). That is a concrete, buildable target rather
than a gesture at ``more data'', and it is the experiment that would separate
our regime claim from the undersampling reading once and for all.

\paragraph{Route on cost tiers, not modes.} Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} shows
that asking only which price band the right mode falls in lifts in-sample
agreement to 89.9--96.7\% using the same inputs (with the caveat recorded
there). That is a smaller decision and a
better-posed one.

\paragraph{Report effect sizes against measured repetition floors.} The most
transferable methodological result here is negative and cheap: on
\cell{cls$\cdot$B0}, adding a different representation was worth no more than
running the same one twice. Any web-agent study reporting single-digit
percentage-point differences without a same-condition replicate is reporting a
number it cannot interpret. The replicate costs one extra run.

\paragraph{Make failure detection cheap.} A policy that starts with the
cheapest mode and escalates only after failure reaches the full ceiling in
success, but at $+316$ to $+463\%$ of the cost of the best single mode,
almost all of it spent confirming that unsolvable tasks are unsolvable. A
cheap, reliable ``this attempt has failed'' detector would turn that overspend
into the prize prediction could not claim. And unlike the question of which
mode to use, this one has an answer on every task, solved or not.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Web agents pay for perception at every step of every task, and the choice of how
much to pay is normally made once and applied everywhere. This dissertation
asked whether that choice should instead be made per task, and whether it can
be.

We built a controlled space of page representations. Six observation modes
vary the page text, the wording of the instructions, and whether an annotated
image is sent; the task, the agent, the actions available and the rules for
counting cost stay fixed. We measured across eight combinations of website and
model, spanning two benchmarks and three models.

\textbf{The opportunity exists and is measurable.} A retrospective oracle gains $+3.45$ to
$+16.35\pp$ of task success over the best single mode at 1.6--35.3\% lower
cost, in every cell and on both benchmarks. \textbf{Most of its height,
however, is repetition}: one plain repeat of an unchanged mode buys
2.0--7.6\pp{} by itself, and six repeats of the strongest single mode take
more than half of what six different modes add over it, and, if tasks flip
like fair coins, come within 3.53\pp{} of matching them, below the threshold
we derive and apply to everyone else's effects. What survives is the ceiling's
price rather than its height: selection reaches that level with one attempt
per task where repetition needs six.

\textbf{The opportunity is not reachable.} Under a protocol in which every
modelling choice is made on training tasks alone, checked against 10,000
shuffles of the outcomes, the learned router is both more successful and
cheaper than the trivial always-cheapest policy in 0 of 8 cells. The
after-the-fact chooser fails the same test in 7 of 8. Always-cheapest is the
cheapest fixed policy on average, though not a per-episode cost floor, which is
how the one remaining oracle cell beats it, so anything else has almost always to
buy enough extra success to cover what it additionally spends, and none of the
deployable policies measured here does. The negative result is therefore about the economics of this boundary,
not about how good the router is at predicting.

\textbf{The reason is a supply constraint, and we can now price it.} A task
can only teach a router which mode to use if some mode solved it. At 2--27\%
base success, 4 of 6 cells do not reach the minimum number of solved tasks per
mode that we require before fitting a classifier, and three separate ways out
of that shortage fail for three different reasons. Even the easier question
(will any mode solve this task at all) is answered largely by an input
no deployment has: the benchmark's own human-written difficulty rating. Training
deliberately on less data confirms the objection's force and bounds it: more
data would buy a better predictor, with returns that flatten as it grows, and
the best a triage predictor could become is the after-the-fact triage chooser,
which itself wins in only 1 of 8 cells. Reaching the stronger after-the-fact
chooser, the one that wins in 7 of 8, would require learning the which-mode
answer as well, and that is precisely the half this section has shown to be
starved of examples.

The evidence is triangulated across three models spanning two design families;
across two benchmarks with different task specifications and annotation
schemas; across both ways we account for cost, money actually
charged and hardware time estimated; and, for the effect sizes the argument
rests on, against a floor measured by rerunning unchanged setups. It is scoped to self-hosted benchmark
environments at 2--36\% base success with task-level routing decisions and a
cost-minimising comparator.

What this work delivers is not a router. It is a measured account of when
representation routing can and cannot pay, and a protocol strict enough that the
answer means something: the opportunity proven to exist before it is declared
unreachable, the failure traced to a mechanism rather than left as an implementation shortfall, and every effect size held against the noise that a
mere repetition would have produced. Those three commitments are what let a
negative result stand as evidence rather than as an absence of it, and they give the next system builder a way to decide, before building, whether their
regime is one in which paying for richer perception selectively could ever have
been worth it.
