\chapter{Predicting Representation Need}
\label{ch:pred}

\chapterhandoff{Chapter~\ref{ch:repr} established a retrospective ceiling and
showed how much of it repetition already buys. The ceiling is computed with
hindsight. This chapter asks whether the same decision can be made
\emph{beforehand}, from signals a deployment would actually have, and is
designed so that the answer, whichever way it falls, cannot be attributed
to the protocol.}

\section{Which half of the problem to attack}
\label{sec:which-half}

Section~\ref{sec:router-formulation} split the oracle into triage (does any mode
solve this task?) and which-mode (given that one does, which?). They differ in
one respect that determines what is even testable.

A task can only say which mode is the right one if some mode actually solved
it; we say such a task \emph{mints} a which-mode label. Every unsolved task
teaches this half of the problem nothing. Table~\ref{tab:supply} shows the
consequence: across the six VisualWebArena cells the number of minted
which-mode labels ranges from 15 to 97, pooled total 260, and after
requiring at least two classes each retaining enough members to survive a
five-fold split, \textbf{4 of 6 cells fail that admissibility rule entirely}.

\begin{scopebox}
\textbf{Say exactly what that rule is, because the stronger phrasing would be
false.} Saying ``no classifier can be trained here'' would be false. What we
actually apply is a minimum we set ourselves before looking: at least 10
training examples in each of at least two classes, once four fifths of the
cell's data are used for training ($n\times\tfrac45 \geq 10$). Falling below
that minimum is not a mathematical impossibility. A logistic regression will
happily fit two classes with three examples each and return a number; the rule
exists to stop us believing that number. The defensible statement is therefore \emph{4 of 6 cells fail the
$\mathrm{min\_class\_n}=10$ rule}, and what it supports is a claim about how much supervision the benchmark produced, not about what an estimator can be made to
output. The same table carries two further caveats: the rule is evaluated against the
\emph{expected} fold size $n\times\tfrac45$ rather than realised fold membership,
so a cell can pass it and still lack a complete five-fold classifier; and the
counts are \emph{minted} labels, of which about 54\% pooled fall in classes that
survive the filter.
\end{scopebox}

The triage label is better posed. It is binary, defined on \emph{every}
task, and available in all 8 cells including the WebArena ones, and it is the
half that Chapter~\ref{ch:repr} showed to be worth up to 30.6\% cost at zero
success loss under an oracle, in 7 of 8 cells. \textbf{We therefore run the predictive study on triage}, deliberately the most favourable framing available: two classes
instead of six, a policy whose oracle version cannot lose success, and a
comparator that is trivially implementable. A negative result here is stronger
than a negative result on the harder half.

\begin{scopebox}
\textbf{But note what triage does \emph{not} escape.} Its positive class is the solvable set, precisely the tasks that mint which-mode labels. The
same solved tasks do double duty. In the which-mode question they are the
entire supply of examples; in the triage question they are the smaller of the
two classes to be told apart. Either way the counts are those in the ``minted
labels'' column of Table~\ref{tab:supply}: 97, 53, 55, 24, 16, 15. Both halves are rationed by the same scarce resource; triage merely
divides it two ways instead of six. So triage is the easier problem, not a problem with more evidence behind it. On \cell{cls$\cdot$B2} the classifier is being asked to learn from 16 positives in 224 rows, roughly three
per fold.
\end{scopebox}

\section{The serving-time feature contract}
\label{sec:features}

A router feature is admissible only if a deployment would possess it at decision
time, before the expensive observation is constructed. Twenty raw features meet
that bar on VisualWebArena:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{Five numeric}: \texttt{dom\_complexity}, \texttt{text\_length},
      \texttt{tokens\_input\_text}, \texttt{intent\_token\_count},
      \texttt{reasoning\_difficulty}.
\item \textbf{Fifteen binary}: \texttt{has\_reference\_image} plus fourteen
      lexical intent predicates matched against the task instruction, namely
      \texttt{intent\_}$\langle$\emph{search, compare, visual\_attribute,
      form\_fill, sort, filter, nav, temporal, color, aggregate, compose,
      question, action\_word, account\_action}$\rangle$. Their definitions are
      in Appendix~\ref{app:router}.
\end{itemize}

All are computable from the task instruction and the cheap structured
observation. None requires running a model, constructing an image, or observing
an outcome. Appendix~\ref{app:router} gives the complete dictionary with
per-feature definitions, together with the exact order of operations inside a
cross-validation fold, so that the leakage controls described below can be
checked rather than taken on trust.

\section{Protocol: what a negative result has to survive}
\label{sec:protocol}

The router is an $L_2$-regularised logistic regression on the 18 raw features,
evaluated by task-held-out five-fold cross-validation per cell, seed 42. The
model is deliberately simple; the interesting design decisions are all in what
is held out.

\paragraph{Full nesting.} The tasks in each cell are split five ways. One
fifth is set aside as test data and not consulted again until the end.
Everything else happens on the remaining four fifths: the inputs are rescaled
using only those rows; the best-performing mode and the cheapest mode are
re-identified from only those rows' success and cost; and the decision
threshold $\tau$ is chosen by splitting those rows again and scoring the part
held back within them. Only then is the model, fitted on training rows alone,
applied to the fifth that was set aside. Nothing that touches a test fold has
seen it.

We previously got this wrong, which is why it is spelled out. An earlier
revision of this analysis called its threshold ``nested'' while in fact
reusing scores computed across the whole cell, scores whose own splits
included the rows being tested, and while choosing the best and cheapest
mode from the whole cell too. The numbers below supersede it.
We record the correction because it changes the interpretation of a headline
number, not to narrate debugging.

\paragraph{One thing nesting exposed.} The best mode is \emph{not stable across
folds}. On \cell{red$\cdot$B0} the five outer folds select \mDOM{}, \mDOM{},
\mSoM{}, \mSoM{}, \mDOM{}. A pipeline that picks one best mode from all realised
outcomes is not merely optimistic about its threshold: it reports a mode choice its own resampling does not reproduce.

\paragraph{Two controls, neither optional.} \emph{(i)} A bundle-permutation
null at $B=10{,}000$: the permutation unit is the whole task bundle
$(y,\,\mathrm{success},\,\mathrm{cost})$ against $X$. Permuting $y$ alone would
disconnect the label from the outcomes that define it, and its error is not
one-directional. \emph{(ii)} The always-cheapest fixed policy as the comparator,
because it is the policy a deployment gets for free.

\paragraph{Baseline sanity check.} We report each cell's best \emph{single}
covariate alongside the fitted model. If the model does not clear it, ``learnable''
means ``learnable by one covariate'', which is not a router.

\section{Why AUROC is not the answer here}
\label{sec:auroc-not-answer}

The one cell that survives, the forum site with the Gemma model
(\cell{red$\cdot$B2}), has an AUROC of 0.526, worse than simply using its
single most informative input, which reaches 0.790. That is not a contradiction; the two
quantities measure different things and this data pulls them apart.

AUROC scores the \emph{global} ranking. The saving comes from the \emph{tail}.
The two come apart hardest exactly where the classes are most lopsided. In a
cell where 93\% of tasks are unsolvable, a model can rank that great mass
sensibly enough to score 0.70 without ever confidently picking out a solvable
one. Picking out the solvable ones is the only thing triage actually needs. A precision--recall curve would separate these two situations
where AUROC does not, and we report AUROC only because it is the quantity the
routing literature reports; the policy tables in Chapter~\ref{ch:e2e}, not this
column, are what the conclusion rests on.
\cell{red$\cdot$B2} routes 192 of 203 tasks (95\%) to the cheap mode with no success loss, in a cell where only 7.4\% of tasks are solvable by anything at
all, so almost nothing in that 95\% was ever going to succeed. It differs from
the free always-cheapest policy on 5\% of the allocation, and those 11 retained
tasks happen to hold 5 of the cell's successes against always-cheapest's 4. What
the shuffling test detects is that this small group is unusually rich in
solvable tasks, not that the model has ranked all the tasks well.

\begin{scopebox}
\textbf{The honest phrasing.} It is \emph{not} ``the label is predictable, yet
triage fails''. It is: \textbf{at 2--36\% base success, a high AUROC is neither
necessary nor sufficient. What decides whether triage saves anything is whether a handful of tail tasks fall on the right side, and at $n=203$ that handful is four successes.}
\end{scopebox}

This has a consequence for how the next chapter must be read. A classifier
metric cannot stand in for a system result in this regime, in either direction:
a cell can discriminate well and buy nothing, and a cell can barely discriminate
and buy something. Chapter~\ref{ch:e2e} therefore evaluates policies, not
scores.

\section{Is this just too few rows?}
\label{sec:undersampling}

Every claim so far invites one reply, and it is the cheapest available way to
dismiss a negative result: \emph{you did not measure a mechanism, you measured a
small sample}. A cell with $n=203$ and 7\% solvable yields 15 positive which-mode labels, and nothing fits. On that reading the whole result is
an artefact of a compute budget rather than a property of the regime.

The objection deserves measurements, not prose, so we ran three, each isolating
a different thing it could mean. Two of the three come back \emph{partly
against} this dissertation, and those two are Figure~\ref{fig:f16}; we report
them that way.

\paragraph{Does more of the same data help? Yes, with decelerating returns.}
We train on progressively smaller samples of the training rows, keeping the
mix of classes the same and leaving each test fold untouched. The resulting
curve isolates the effect of training size alone. Out-of-fold AUROC rises by $+0.032$
to $+0.083$ between a quarter of the data and all of it. It is not flat: more
rows would help. But the increments shrink: in 9 of 12 curves the sweep from
55\% to 100\% buys less than the sweep from 25\% to 55\%, despite covering the
wider span of the two. Extrapolating the observed deceleration, doubling the
benchmark again would be worth a few hundredths of AUROC, not a few tenths.

\paragraph{Is there signal at all? Yes.} A near-unregularised model
($C=10^{6}$) fitted to all rows and scored on those same rows separates them
better than the same model fitted to \emph{permuted} labels does: an excess of
$+0.075$ to $+0.116$ AUROC over the memorisation floor, $p \leq 0.01$ in five of
six cells. The features are not noise. \textbf{So we cannot dispose of the
objection by claiming there is nothing to learn}: there is something, and a larger sample would learn more of it.

\paragraph{And yet it does not reach the result, for a reason that has nothing
to do with sample size.} More data moves the learned predictor upward from
AUROC $0.73$; the limit of that movement is a perfect predictor, AUROC $=1.00$.
A perfect predictor of the triage label \emph{is} the oracle triage policy, and Chapter~\ref{ch:e2e} measures what that policy achieves. It
is both more successful and cheaper than always-cheapest in \textbf{1 of 8
cells}. The learned router manages 0 of 8, and
the entire distance between them is at most one cell.

\begin{scopebox}
\textbf{The precise scope of the objection.} Undersampling explains the gap
between AUROC $0.73$ and AUROC $1.00$. It cannot explain a boundary that the
policy at AUROC $1.00$ also fails to cross. Since this dissertation rests on the Pareto result and not on the AUROC value, \textbf{the objection
is correct about the predictor and irrelevant to the conclusion}. Conversely,
any future work that reports a better router on this problem should be asked
which of the two it improved.
\end{scopebox}

\section{What this chapter establishes}
\label{sec:ch5-summary}

The which-mode half of routing cannot be attempted under our own minimum-data
rule: 4 of 6 cells do not reach the required number of solved tasks per mode,
and what constrains them is the supply of solved tasks
(Chapter~\ref{ch:disc}).

The triage half, the easier half by construction, is weakly predictable,
at AUROC 0.526--0.758, beating its single most informative input in 5 of 8
cells. But
most of the apparent predictability on VisualWebArena rests on two features no
deployment possesses, and after removing them five of six cells fall. Under a
$B=10{,}000$ bundle-permutation null with Holm correction across the eight
